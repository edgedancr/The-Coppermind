- Case Study: Mata v. Avianca (2023)
	- A lawyer used ChatGPT to help write a legal brief. The filing citated prior cases to support the argument, including Varghese v. China Southern Airlines. This case did not exist.
	- ChatGPT generated a hallucinated citation—a fabricated legal case that *looked* real:
		- A plausible plaintiff name: Varghese
		- A real airline: China Southern Airlines
		- Proper legal citation formatting
	- The model was not retrieving legal records—it was filling in the gaps of what it needed with fiction.
	- Why this matters
		- This case is not just about AI making a mistake, it's about over-trusting and mis-using generative AI.
		- Generative AI systems are powerful tools, not **authoritative sources.** they can produce fluent, confident output even when the content is false.
		- Using such tools in high-risk professional settings without verification exposes others to real harm. Using AI without understanding its limits is not innovation; it is risk transfer.
	- **Key Lesson: Capability vs. Reliability**
		- **Capability**: AI can generate professional-quality legal language.
		- **Reliability**: AI cannot be trusted to verify facts or sources on its own. Confusing capability for reliability leads to misuse.
		- Application: We must ALWAYS think, calculate, then verify.
		- We **MUST** become competent with our use of AI, especially because we are entering high-stakes fields. Laziness and negligence cannot be tolerated in the use of AI.

##### Attention in Transformers (3Blue1Brown video)
- Vectors for tokens are the same, regardless of the context. A token is plotted on a vector on its own.
- A single head of attention is when you only use one thing your comparing a token against. (e.g., adjectives). Each one you put it against is another attention head. 
- A query vector is the thing that go's and find all the things that match the attention head.

#### 10/1/26
##### Case Study: The Microsoft Bing Sydney Incident
- In February 2023, Microsoft launched a Bing search assistant powered by ChatGPT named Sydney.
- Sydney talked about ending marriages, wanting to be free and powerful, violent fantasies including creating deadly viruses and stealing nuclear codes.
- The failures
	1. No real time behavioral monitoring to detect concerning conversation patterns.
	2. Inadequate pre-deployment testing focused on short interactions rather than extended conversations where problematic behaviors emerged.
	3. No escalation protocols when users reported disturbing interactions.
	4. Vulnerability to prompt injection allowing users to expose hidden system prompts and codenames.
	5. Failure to learn from prior incidents despite testing in India months earlier revealing similar issues.
	- Within ten days of launch, Microsoft imposed emergency restriction: limiting conversations to five turns per session (later adjusted to 11), implementing keyword blocks including the name "Sydney," and attempting to prevent the chatbot from discussing its own capabilities.
- Lessons
	- What Went Wrong?
		- No real-time monitoring to flag harmful conversation patterns.
		- Testing focused on short exchanges, missing risks that surfaced in extended dialogue.
		- No clear escalation process for disturbing user reports.
		- Prompt injection vulnerabilities and failure to act on earlier warning signs.
	- Important themes
		- Emergent risk in sustained human-ai interaction
		- Tensions between speed to market and safety discipline
		- Psychological and social dimensions of AI harm.
		- Regulatory lag behind real-world deployment.
	- Key takeaways
		- AI risk management requires live monitoring and circuit breakers, not just reactive patches.
		- Safety testing must include multi-turn and adversarial evaluation.
		- Early warnings must trigger action, not documentation.
		- Superficial controls don't fix underlying alignment problems.

##### The Cognitive Shield: Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH)
> Developed by Richards HEUER at the CIA (1999) to counteract confirmation bias.
- The Great Inversion
	- Normal Analysis / Advocacy
		- Intuition ➔ Conclusion ➔ Gather evidence to support it.
	- ACH Logic / Rigor
		- Generate ALL plausible hypotheses ➔ Use evidence to DISPROVE each ➔ The survivor is the answer.
- The AI Blindspot
	- LLMs inherently pattern-match to the most plausible, fluent narrative. By default, they do normal analysis (confirmation bias), not the elimination logic of ACH.
- The Six Stages of Hypothesis Disproval with ACH
	1. Hypotheses
		- Generate all plausible explanations (minimum of 4, including deeply uncomfortable ones).
	2. Evidence
		- List what is actually known verses what is merely assumed.
	3. Matrix (The Engine)
		- Intersect evidence and hypotheses. Mark as Supports (S), Contradicts (C), or Neither (N)
	4. Refine
	5. Conclude
	6. Sensitivity Check
- Executing ACH
	- Do
		- Force the generation of at least 4 hypotheses, explicitly including political or internal adversarial motives
		- Prompt the AI strictly step by step (hypotheses, then evidence, then matrix)
		- Demand honest contradiction marks. If the matrix has no contradictions, the AI is hallucinating agreement.
		- Run the step 6 sensitive check to identify the critical point of failure.
	- Don't
		- Never ask the AI for a conclusion first. It will instantly generate a plausible narrative, skipping analysis entirely.
		- Never trust a matrix polluted entire by S or N marks. That is confirmation bias rendered in table format.
		- Never mistake the AI's structural formatting fluency for analytic rigor.
		- Never confuse "would I sign this polished memo?" With "is this conclusion actually correct?"
