> Lowenthal Chapter 2: The Development of US Intel

1. *Intelligence Community*: A superstructure of agencies and offices intended to work together to provide the most effective and actionable intel product. **17 Agencies/Orgs – See Org/Msns**

2. *National Intelligence*: initially describing foreign intelligence, the term now encompasses all of the IC's intelligence, including domestic and homeland security. Includes all intelligence, including foreign, domestic, and homeland security interests.

3. *Competitive Analysis*: several agencies working on the same issue to bring in different perspectives, weed out bias, and leave only proximate reality remaining; approach to increase the likelihood that the product will reflect reality.

4. *Groupthink*: valuing conformity of opinion over objective analysis.

5. *National technical means*: a nation's technological methods and tools, such as satellites, for collecting intelligence, not usually operated by a tactical military organization.

6. *Verification*: the ability to ascertain whether or not treaty obligations were being met or if there is cheating in some way to the agreement.

7. *Key Judgements*: the central **conclusions** in a national intelligence estimate

8. *National Security Act,1947* 32: Created the modern U.S. national security apparatus – NSC, JCS, with a CIA with a charter to “perform such other functions” as directed.

9. *IRTPA, 2004* 33 (31; 38): First major revision of the National Security Act of 1947, and created the DNI as separate from any intel agency, and redesignated the director of the CIA as the DCIA.

10. *ICD’s* 33: Directives issued by the DNI establishing policies for the IC.

Lowenthal Chapter 3: The Intelligence Community

1. *NIP* 39, 61, 63 (33): the National Intelligence Program, which funds several civilian intelligence agencies and departments under the designation of the DNI.

2. *MIP* 39, 61, 63 (33): the Military Intelligence Program, which funds intelligence programs within the DoD that support tactical, military operations.

3. *NIPF* 42 (36): the National Intelligence Priorities Framework, which is a five-tier mechanism by which the NSC and DNI designate priorities for the intelligence community to ensure that the products created are relevant for policymakers and intel users.

4. *Principals Committee* 51 (43): the senior policy coordinating body in the NSC, where top officials, including the secretaries of defense and state, meet to discuss intelligence issues.

5. *USDI* 53 (47): the undersecretary of defense for intelligence. This is the undersecretary within the DoD that handles day-to-day intelligence issues. They report to the Secretary of Defense, but are also under the DNI in activities related to EXCOM, and are one of the two main sources of intelligence for Congress.

6. *Crosswalks* 62 (53): efforts to coordinate program (***resource/investment***) focus in order to operate most effectively under budget constraints.

7. *Supplementals* 64 (55): special, one-year appropriations by Congress above the general budget for agreed upon priorities. Unlike the general budget, these appropriations are not long-term commitments.

Lowenthal Chapter 4: US Intel Process

1. *Requirements* 67-72 (57): defining which policy projects the IC is expected to contribute to, their relative priority, and potentially **which collection methods** they are to use.

2. *Collection*: 72-73 (57; 62): The process of gathering raw information to be processed and distilled into intelligence. Not to be conflated with *intelligence*, which has undergone the processing and exploitation.

3. *Processing and exploitation*: 73-74 (57): the process of turning raw information into intelligence for analysis. One example would be translating acquired documents into a language that the analysts can read.

4. *Analysis and Production*: 74-75 (58): the process of sorting and publishing intelligence into its various products according to its priority and level of detail.

5. *Priority creep*: 71 (61): the tendency for analysts and policymakers to ***push for their issue*** to take greater precedence in the NIPF, where they end up competing with projects already in that priority bracket.

6. *Tyranny of the ad hocs*: 72 (62): the prevalence of surprise issues to be *placed in **higher priority***, diverting attention to projects already in progress (one such example would be a SNIE)

7. *Footnote Wars*: 75 (64): the practice of citing sources for the sole purpose of maintain **independent points of view**, regardless of whether it is relevant to the issue at hand.

***Discussion Questions***

1. *How does Lowenthal define intelligence*? When he states that intelligence is *not about ‘truth*,’ what is the larger idea he is emphasizing?
    Intelligence is information that has been narrowed to meet the needs of policymakers. However, this information is rarely so certain as "truth." Rather, it is an estimate, a projection, that analysts believe approximates reality. He is emphasizing that intelligence is not certain, it is our best, closest answer.

2. *What should be the relationship between the National Security Strategy and the National Intelligence Strategy*?
    The National Security Strategy should outline aspirational goals, then clear objectives/benchmarks to define and limit those goals, such as hardening the electrical grid by so-and-so year. The Intelligence Strategy should outline the necessary IC posture and strategy to gather and provide the intelligence related to those benchmarks to the policymakers.

3. List and describe the **Mission Objectives of the National Intelligence Strategy**:

    1. **Strategic** Intelligence: products that are intended to provide **deep understanding** of a given policymaking topic.

    2. **Anticipatory** Intelligence: intelligence that warns of **changing conditions** and possible future threats to national security before they materialize.

    3. **Current** Operations: providing **timely** intelligence that are **responsive to** and can assist operations and objectives that are in-progress.

    4. **Cyber** Intelligence: developing a better **understanding** of our **adversaries'** cyber capabilities and intentions, as well as their potential impact on national security.

    5. **Counterterrorism**: continuing to **identify, understand, and disrupt** parties attempting to terrorize targets that would be detrimental to national security interests.

    6. **Counterproliferation**: continuing to identify ways of **preventing** hostile actors from acquiring **WMDs**, WMD delivery mechanisms and technical expertise.

    7. **Counterintelligence**: **mitigating attempts** by hostile intelligence entities, both public and private, **to undermine US** economic strength and national security.

**HAI Week 3 Intelligence Analysis**

**Lowenthal Chapter 6: Analysis**

1. **Current intelligence vs. long term intelligence:** 154 (122). Current intelligence will only be relevant for one or two days or weeks. Long term reports address issues on the more distant time horizon, and usually with greater depth and context. Generally, policymakers prefer to read current intelligence, and analysts prefer to produce long term reports.

2. **Mirror-imaging:** 163 (129). the mistaken belief that other actors, such as leaders or states, will behave as the analyst would in a given situation.

3. *Competitive analysis* 169-172, 184-185 (136). Having different agencies with different points of view work on the same problem. This may include Red Teams, teams with decidedly different viewpoints (Teams A and B), or other approaches designed to survive the bureaucratic processes designed to emphasize consensus.

4. **Groupthink:** 168 (135). lack of diverse opinions on an issue that stems from a failure to properly challenge and analyze the presuppositions; a premature and inappropriate degree of unanimity on a subject.

5. **Metaphors for intelligence:** 172 (139). illustrations of the intelligence process, and some are better than others; Includes connecting the dots, assembling a picture piece by piece, or assembling a mosaic, a string of pearls.

6. **Confidence levels*: 176-7 (143).* ranging from low to high confidence, these terms refer to the degree of confidence analysts place in the scope and quality of the information that they are basing their judgements on in intelligence reports.
    *-High confidence* are judgements based on high-quality information, or the nature of the issue makes solid judgement possible.
    *-Moderate confidence* is when the available information can be interpreted many ways, or there are alternate views, or the information is credible, but not sufficiently corroborated.
    *-Low confidence* is when the information is scant, questionable, or fragmented, making solid inferences difficult; or is based on possibly problematic sources*.*

7. **Politicized intelligence:** 186 (151). intel that is ***biased* toward a preferred outcome**, either of the decision makers or the analysts; offering recommendations may be viewed as ‘crossing a line’ into the policy realm, depending on the circumstances.

8. **Good intel:** 194-5 (157-8). intel that helps decision makers make good decisions. It is Timely, Tailored, Digestable, with Knowns and Unknowns clearly stated.

> **Lowenthal Chapter 9: Role of the Policymaker**

1. **NSC:** 255-6 (199-200). The National Security Council consisting of the President, VP, Secretaries of Energy, Defense, and State Department, and the chairman of JCS. The DNI acts as a subordinate intelligence advisor to the NSC. The NSC has both an executive advisory role to the President and role in implementing the President’s security priorities.

2. **Inter-agency process:** 256-7 (201). The process of all involved Executive Branch agencies arriving at consensus on policy recommendations or implementation actions through bargaining or negotiating.

3. *Policy maker behaviors* 270-3. (*PBD Webinar* emphasized some of this…)
    The different idiosyncrasies of policy makers in regard to consumption of intelligence. Some prefer to read while others prefer to be briefed. They are best served if they convey their preferences instead of leaving them to guess work.

4. *Tensions-Policy Makers & IC*: 273-4. Decision makers want to use intel to *support on-going actions*, while IC members have a vested interest in *not revealing sources and methods* to the targets, which taking action based on the intel could do. Additionally, *external intrusions* from entities such as the media can create tension by allowing policy makers to mistake their coverage for the same as the IC. These are causes for tension between the IC and decision makers at all levels.

**Lowenthal Chapter 11 intel agenda: Nation-States**

1. **Containment:** 320 (252). The strategy, first propagated by George Kennan, that the Soviet Union should be contained within its geographic limits. Its premise was that the Soviet system must expand to survive, and that by containing it geographically, the US could force it to adapt or collapse.

2. **Maskirovka:** 322 (254). Russia’s tradition of hiding its realities to confuse or mislead outside observers.

3. **Bean counting:** 323 (255). Intelligence that quantifies and catalogs physical military assets of a target nation (e.g., number of missiles, army divisions, or planes); often a disparaging term.

4. **Capabilities vs. intentions:** 324 (256). Reference to whether the military capabilities of a foreign power or its intentions should be the focus of intel and of defense and resource planning.

5. **Worst-case analysis:** 325 (257). An assessment that assumes the worst possible situation is likely to arise and so should be the basis for defense planning.

6. **Reagan Doctrine:** 329 (261). The policy of providing military assistance to guerilla fighters that opposed the Soviet Union and Soviet-allied countries, such as leftist governments in South America to throw the Soviet Union off balance.

7. **Failed states:** 334 (265). States that are unable to provide basic public order or services to the populace and can no long control its own territory.

**Lowenthal Chapter 12 The Intelligence Agenda: Transnational Issues**

1. **CNE vs. CNA:** 351, 353 (275).
    -Computer Network Exploitation is retrieving data or taking operational control of a target computer;
    -Computer Network Attack is disrupting or bringing down the relevant network.
    Intel analysis often involves determining whether it will be better to exploit access by collecting data or taking control of the infiltrated network (CNE) or to destroy the network or disrupt the command and control of enemy resources (CNA).

2. **BDA:** 351 (276). Battle Damage Assessment is a measure of how successful an attack was by assessing its effects on the target using all sources available.

3. Attribution 353. The ability to determine or attribute a particular cyber attack to an actor. This is made difficult by the ambiguous nature of cyber warfare, especially when trying to attribute the actions of non-state actors.

4. *Zero-day vulnerabilities* 357. This refers to flaws in hardware or software that are not generally known to the public and that can be exploited by an attacker. In the USA, NSA often exploits some of these as part of its operations and so wants to hold this info closely.

5. *Intel support in Economic domain* 378-382. The intelligence community supports the economic domain by conducting foreign economic espionage, monitoring threats to the supply chain, forecasting major economic shifts, monitoring competition for materials and oil and natural gas.

6. *FININT* 382. Financial intelligence, a part of economic intelligence that follows money flows, especially from illicit or illegal activities. Due to the modern economy largely transfering money digitally, FININT often shades into cyber intelligence.

7. **RMA:** 387 (301). Revolution in military affairs, which refers to the evolution of military technology, doctrine, strategy, and tactics as planners attempt to *prepare for continued changes* in the future of warfare.

> **Kaiser & Pherson 4 Detailed Outline:**

1. **Analytic titles:** (21). Pithy titles with **action verbs grab attention & encapsulate** the basic message of the paper. If the paper cannot be reduced to such a statement, the thesis may not have been narrowed enough.

2. **Key judgements:** (23). Core conclusions of the paper, one step of conceptual unpacking deeper than the title itself, but less robust than the product's full analysis. They are the most important points of an analytic work or things of special interest to the reader.

3. **Topic sentences:** (25). These concisely state the summary of each paragraph. They convey the most important information, following the BLUF format.

***Lowenthal Chapter 5 Collection & the Collection Disciplines***

1\. **All-source** intel 86 (74). It is the “ideal” intel product for the IC. It combines intel from as many sources as possible to account for each source’s shortcomings and to benefit from their combined strength.

2\. **Wheat vs chaff** 87. Or “noise vs signals.” It refers to intelligence collection as a sort of vacuum cleaner, which requires a great deal of sorting to obtain the desired information.

3\. **Processing and Exploitation Imbalance** 87. Because intel production involves a massive amount of daily collection, some important intel will always be overlooked in theory. Not everything that is collected can be exploited, thus the IC is finding ways to capitalize only on the most vital intel.

4\. **Collection swarm ball** 90. When a vast amount of energy and manpower is spent on one problem that is deemed high priority. Often, these swarm balls offer no added benefit but merely distract from other issues. Like a children’s soccer game in which all players swarm around the ball regardless of their assigned positions.

5\. **Classifications**: Confidential, Secret, Top Secret 91 (78) Confidential information is that which might cause damage to national security if it were disclosed. Secret information is expected is cause serious damage to nat’l security if disclosed. Top secret is expected to cause exceptionally grave damage to nat’l security.

6\. **Denial** 95-96 (82) When one nation gains insight into the collection methods of an opponent, and therefore **learns to evade** that collection process.

7\. **Deception** 95-96 (82) After gaining insight into an opponent’s collection process, a nation **can transmit information** through that system. This information can be true or false.

8\. **SIGINT vs IMINT** 116. IMINT (now called GEOINT) essentially tells you what has happened, while SIGINT generally tells you what may happen in the future.

9\. ***Quantum cryptography*** 118Uses light (photons) rather than radio waves. The sender of these signals will know if an interception attempt has been made, and the message cannot be hacked because it self-destructs upon being intercepted.

10\. **Collection disciplines**: Names, strengths, weaknesses 143-4 (115)

> GEOINT **Geo-spatial Intelligence**. (strengths): Graphic and compelling, familiar to policy makers, ready availability, can be done remotely. (weaknesses): Overly graphic, requires interpretation, expensive, very static, subject to weather and other problems.
>
> SIGINT **Signals Intelligence**. (strengths): insight into plans and intentions, voluminous material, can be done remotely. (weaknesses): expensive, voluminous material, signals may be encrypted.
>
> MASINT **Measurements and Signatures Intelligence**. (strengths): useful for proliferation, can be done remotely. (weaknesses): expensive, hard to understand, requires a great deal of processing.
>
> HUMINT **Human Intelligence**. (strengths): insights into plans and intentions, inexpensive. (weaknesses): Risks lives and political fallout, requires more time, problems with agents.
>
> OSINT **Open Source Intelligence**. (strengths): readily available, place to start all collection. (weaknesses): voluminous, less likely to offer insights from clandestine INTs.
>
> FININT **Financial Intelligence**. —Useful for uncovering monetary relationships and can be done remotely; can take time to make impact.

11\. **GEO orbit** 93 (80) Geosynchronous orbit, or 22,000 miles.

12\. **HEO orbit** 93 (80) Highly elliptical orbit, very low over southern hemisphere (300 miles) and very high over northern hemisphere (25,000 miles).

13\. **LEO orbit** 93 (80) Low earth orbit, or roughly 200-1000 miles.

14\. **MEO orbit** 93 (80) Medium earth orbit refers to the space between LEO and GEO orbits.

**Lowenthal Chapter 7 Counterintelligence**

1. **Counterintelligence** (163) Defined by EO 12333 as “information gathered and activities conducted to identify, deceive, exploit, disrupt or protect” against espionage and other activities by foreign states or non-state adversaries. It includes: collection, defensive, and offensive intel operations. The three types of counterintelligence include: collection, defensive, and offensive.
    - Collection: gaining information about an opponent’s intelligence collection capabilities
    -.Defensive: thwarting efforts by hostile intelligence services to penetrate one’s own servicec.
    - Offensive: once an opponent’s efforts are identified, manipulating these attacks by turning the opponent’s agents into double agents or by feeding them false info

2\. **Damage assessment** 2151. Damage assessment occurs when a spy has been identified. It is analysis run by the intelligence community that looks into what intelligence has been compromised. The cooperation of the spy can be used as a negotiating point to decrease the degree of punishment.

3\. **Insider threat** 2171. Insider threats are a reality in counterintelligence, as many of the United States’ greatest spies or traitors were homegrown. A 2011 Executive Order by President Obama created the Insider Threat Program to deter, detect, and mitigate insider threats while both improving security and allowing the sharing of classified information.

4\. **National security letters** 2231. National security letters are a type of administrative subpoena. They are an investigative technique that require “recipients to turn over records and data pertaining to individuals, with the added proviso of a gag order – the recipient of the NSL may not reveal its contents or even the ***fact of*** its existence.”

5. **ORCON control** (167) A classification marking reflecting agency “ownership” of the data and that requires further distribution of that intelligence or including it in another document to be approved by the original agency. Since supplemented by Mike McConnell’s recommendation that there is a ‘***responsibility-to-provide***.’ **IMPLICATIONS?** May defeat the purpose of intel sharing, by *slowing down* flow of info; at the same time *protect ‘sources and methods*.’

Intelligence reporting on terrorist threats began to **increase dramatically** in the spring of 2001, beyond the IC’s capability to digest all of the info on the terrorist organizations and figure out what it might mean.
A report in May noted that operatives might try to hijack an aircraft or storm an embassy. *In June* reports said al Qaeda activity that suggested attack planning had “*reached a crescendo*,” estimating July to be the attack date. The estimated target was Israel or the Arabian Peninsula. *An August* article to president Bush was labeled “*Bin Ladin ***Determined to Strike U.S***.*,” but there doesn’t seem to have been any further discussion before 9/11 about a possible strike on American soil. Knew the threat was developing; didn’t have ***TACTICAL INTEL*** to tell us where it was targeted.

3\. **Artificial intelligence** (Kelnar) AI is a general term referring to hardware and/or software exhibiting intelligent behavior. AI exists via rule-based programs that deliver displays of “intelligence.” In AI, the subset of “machine learning” reflects rapid and significant advances. These advances allow AI to learn without having to be programmed.

4\. **Fourth industrial revolution** (Kelnar) The processing of information enables us to do more intelligent work. As a result, machine learning will help software dominate the world at large. This will echo the three previous industrial revolutions that transformed production through new sources of power.

5\. **Internet of Things** (loT) (Kelnar) A system of computing devices that are provided with unique identifiers. Also, these computing devices have the ability to transfer data over a network that requires either a human-to-human interaction or a human-to-computer interaction.

6\. **Cyber security** (Lowenthal 156-157, 24-278) The rising new threat that needs to be managed is Cyber security (cyberspace). There are four major areas of concern that are addressed in SMO: infrastructure links, cyber espionage, denial-of-service attacks, cyber-based terrorism. Due to the level of newness in this domain, there is still an ambiguous doctrine of response for policymakers in cyber security.

7\. **Computer network attack** (**CNA**) (Lowenthal 275-276) When reasons are necessitated to bring down the network and to disrupt command & control of the network for a user’s own needs. This can include destructive attacks, temporary interruptions, or modification of data (e.g., replace one video with another).

8\. **Computer network exploitation** (**CNE**) (Lowenthal 275, 277) When one achieves access to a threat’s computer, the ability to exploit the access determining the capabilities, collect data, and take control of the network all falls under CNE.

1\. *AI singularity* (1)- AI singularity is when an Artificial Intelligence surpasses human intelligence and most importantly, operates without human control. This means the AI is able to operate and make its own decisions without outside humans stopping or controlling it.

2\. *Digital authoritarian state techniques* (1-2)-Authoritarian states can censor through several different ways, including predictive control of dissenters through AI, censoring speech retroactively, and using the extensive amount of information available to them online, for example, medical and criminal records.

3\. *Wild West in political advertising* (4)-The “Wild West” is the absence of regulation in political advertising. It has resulted in an extremely “wild” market within advertising, most significantly been the use of user info to target and direct advertising. The extensiveness on this was seen through the absence of labels on advertising and the extremely loose definition of “publisher” as defined by United States law.

c\) **What are some of the implications for Intelligence Community people, processes, technology, and organization of these emerging technologies?**
As we grow more closer to the use of AI to SMO, the nature of military itself will drastically change. Now the new Air Force commander is concerned with technological weaknesses and cyber attacks rather than a foot invasion off the coast of Florida. This influx of AI and cyberspace is necessitating the private sector to supply the demands of the IC for more effective and competitive responses to problems nation-states pose. As a result, the likelihood of another industrial revolution appears to be within 30 years down the horizon. These changes further suggest a dynamic 1-2 generations down the road will see: a revamping of military branches based on the new AI and cyber fronts of threats.
