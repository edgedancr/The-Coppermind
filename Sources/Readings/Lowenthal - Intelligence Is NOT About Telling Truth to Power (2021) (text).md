Intelligence is NOT About “Telling Truth
to Power”
Intelligence officers love to explain their role in the policy process as
“telling truth to power.” They wrap themselves in this phrase; it makes
them feel noble. You can see a similar sentiment when you walk into
the original CIA headquarters, in marble on the wall on the left: “And
ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.
—John VIII:32.”

It is a lovely sentiment. It is also wrong and it is dangerous.
The original phrase, “speaking truth to power,” is credited to Bayard
Rustin, the civil rights leader, who wrote in 1942 that this was the role of a
religious group. Rustin was a Quaker. In 1955, “Speak Truth to Power” was
the title of a Quaker pamphlet advocating nonviolence. Indeed, this phrase
has long been a clarion call for those pursuing nonviolent means of
protesting or changing government policies.
It is not clear how or when this phrase came to be adopted by intelligence
professionals, but it is somewhat ironic. Intelligence is not necessarily
violent—although operations can be—but intelligence is certainly an
instrument of state power. However, as the inscription at Central Intelligence
Agency headquarters shows, the idea of intelligence as truth has been with us

for many decades. The concept of intelligence as truth was confirmed in
2017, in the director of national intelligence’s “Principles of Professional
Ethics for the Intelligence Community.” The second principle is: “Truth: We
seek the truth; speak truth to power; and obtain, analyze, and provide
intelligence objectively.”
The idea of intelligence as truth is wrong because it misrepresents the very
nature of the intelligence we write and give to policymakers and what our
goals are in doing so. We rarely have a complete picture of a given situation.
There are always questions about our sources, their currency, and their
reliability—in addition to determining what the sources actually say. As the
January 2017 Intelligence Community Assessment (ICA) on Russian
activities in the 2016 election notes, intelligence analysis seeks “to reduce the
uncertainty surrounding foreign activities, capabilities, or leaders’ intentions.
This objective is difficult to achieve when seeking to understand complex
issues on which foreign actors go to extraordinary lengths to hide or
obfuscate their activities.”
This concept of uncertainty is crucial to understanding the true nature of
intelligence. When I teach the art and craft of analysis to budding analysts,
one of the points that I stress over and over is the importance of conveying to
policymakers your certainty and uncertainty—especially your uncertainty.
Policymakers will be using this analysis as one of the factors when they make
decisions. If the analysis is over-written—that is, if it overstates the
certainty—then policymakers are more likely to make bad decisions—and the
fault will lie with the intelligence analyst.
If the Intelligence Community (IC) recognizes and emphasizes the issue of
uncertainty, how then do we also claim to be providing truth? We want
policymakers to use intelligence advisedly and correctly, not assuming that all
is known, which is rarely the case. If intelligence is about truth, then there
can be no uncertainties. Truth is an absolute.
It can be difficult to convey this uncertainty to policymakers in ways that
they will understand and still give them a sense of the underlying intelligence.
I recognized this as an issue in the aftermath of the 2003 National
Intelligence Estimate (NIE) on Iraq weapons of mass destruction when I was
the assistant director of central intelligence (DCI) for analysis and a vice
chairman of the National Intelligence Council. I asked Robert Walpole, one
of the national intelligence officers who wrote the NIE, if we had ever created
a dictionary or guide to help policymakers understand estimative language.
Walpole said we had tried—and failed—three times. I told Walpole to try
again. He wrote “What We Mean When We Say: An Explanation of
Estimate Language.” Walpole’s paper was successful and influential, having
been used several times over in IC analyses—including the 2017 ICA noted
above. Walpole laid out a scale of analytic judgments, ranging from

“Remote” to “Almost Certainly.” “Never” and “Always” do not appear
because, if either of these cases occurred, then there would be no need for
intelligence analysis. In other words, we will most often be in a situation
where we cannot be absolutely sure—just the opposite of truth.
In January 2015, Director of National Intelligence James Clapper signed
Intelligence Community Directive 203, creating analytic standards. This
includes a range of probabilities very similar to Walpole’s. Again, no
“Never,” no “Always,” and no truth.
The entire intelligence analytic process also points in a direction other than
truth. Most intelligence analysis is the product of more than one analyst or
more than one agency. There will be disagreements and possibly
compromises. How does this comport with the truth? Can truth be the
outcome of compromise among strongly held views? Are the analysts or
agencies on the losing side of an intelligence debate now guilty of falsehood?
Another problem with the phrase “speaking truth to power” is a more
philosophical one. The phrase is arrogant.
By claiming to be telling the truth to policymakers, intelligence officers
misrepresent their intelligence analyses, allowing policymakers to put more
reliance on them than may be warranted. This is dangerous and can lead to
mistaken policy decisions. After all, if you cannot trust the truth, what can
you trust?
Policymakers are free to accept or reject intelligence as they see fit, for any
number of reasons. They may have information that intelligence officers do
not have and that the policymakers have not shared. This happens more
often than one might assume. Policymakers will have contacts and meetings
to which intelligence officers are not privy. Therefore, the policymakers may
see flaws or inaccuracies in the intelligence. They may reject the intelligence
simply because they disagree, or because they find the intelligence
discomforting—again for any number of reasons. The analysis may be
difficult to believe on some emotional or intellectual level. It may suggest that
a preferred policy has little chance of success. Whatever the reason,
intelligence can be rejected by policymakers.
What are the implications of policymakers rejecting this “truthful”
intelligence? If intelligence is truth and the policymaker rejects it, are they
committing falsehood or heresy? Intelligence is never ex cathedra. Good
analysts should always keep in the back of their minds Oliver Cromwell’s
challenge, “ … think it possible you might be mistaken.” Intelligence analysts
who claim they are “truth tellers” should also keep in mind that in medieval
courts it was the court jester who was allowed to tell truth to the king—not
the role to which intelligence officers should aspire.

Honesty in intelligence analysis admits to fallibility, as difficult as that

It is past time that intelligence officers stop using the phrase, “Telling truth
to power.” It is inaccurate, it is arrogant, and, frankly, we do not need it. We
perform a useful function for policymakers at many levels throughout the
