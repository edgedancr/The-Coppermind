> ABSTRACT
>
> Analytic objectivity as a standard for the US Intelligence Community appears to have been drawn from idealized conceptualizations of the scientific method as a kind of value-neutral epistemological framework used to develop knowledge “objectively.” But this embrace of objectivity provides a poor foundation for contemporary conceptualizations of the applied epistemology of intelligence analysis, as well as performance standards. Instead, intelligence analysts should embrace a more realistic goal of aspiring to but never actually achieving analytic objectivity through the reduction of subjectivity while embracing the values of honesty, neutrality, and integrity encapsulated in the phrase “calling it as you see it”.

The US Intelligence Community (IC) has established ***unattainable standards*** for its intelligence analysts, reflecting normative assumptions about what the role and function of the intelligence analyst should be. According to the analytic standards articulated in Intelligence Community Directive 203 (ICD 203), intelligence analysts are required to ‘*perform their functions with objectivity*.’ These standards establish the minimum expectations for professional products and practices; by definition standards are not aspirational. *Analytic objectivity in particular is highly valued* because it is seen as the most effective way *to reduce distortion* of the analytic product resulting from subjectivity or bias, which in extreme cases can result in ‘analytic politicization’ or ‘intelligence to please.’

An evaluation of the history of the IC’s efforts to achieve analytic objectivity demonstrates how ***difficult*** the proposition has been over the decades, and at the same time how ***crucial*** it is to the combined identity and ethos of the analyst. The IC’s emphasis on analytic objectivity addresses issues associated with acquisition of truth in an intelligence context ***but*** **embed assumptions about knowledge construction and utilization that do not hold up under historical and conceptual scrutiny**.

Specifically, it appears that ***analytic objectivity is not and cannot be attained***. Objectivity as a construct is *different from *independence*, *neutrality*, or *honesty**. Instead, objectivity is defined just as much by its antonym, subjectivity, with subjectivity reflecting the presence of bias and objectivity reflecting its absence. Efforts to achieve objectivity entail the reduction of subjectivity or bias, but absolute objectivity, defined as the *absence of bias, is an unattainable goal*, and even aspirations to it are asymptotic in practice. Given the inevitable presence of bias, *analysts are not going to be able to ‘perform their functions with objectivity*’ ensuring that intelligence analysts are inevitably deficient in achieving their performance standard.

This embrace of **analytic objectivity** as a standard in ICD 203 appears to have been *drawn from idealized conceptualizations of the relationship between information, knowledge, and decision* that existed *in the sciences* decades ago, with an emphasis on the scientific method as a kind of valueneutral epistemological framework used to develop knowledge ‘objectively.’ But *even in the sciences* this embrace of analytic objectivity within the broader practices of scientific inquiry has come under

# Analytic objectivity and its formalization through ICD 203

Analytic objectivity is a standard promulgated by the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) in the 2000s. ODNI ‘Intelligence Community Directives’ (or ICDs) ‘are the principal issuance through which the DNI provides policy direction to the Intelligence Community (IC).’² According to ODNI, ‘analytic standards govern the production and evaluation of national intelligence analysis. The standards are intended to guide the writing of intelligence analysis in all Intelligence Community (IC) analytic elements.’³ Per the guidance in the 2015 version of ICD 203 on Analytic Standards, ‘all IC analytic products shall be consistent with the following five Analytic Standards.’⁴ The use of the word ‘shall’ is an imperative, denoting obligation or command. As such, these standards establish the minimum expectations for professional products and practices; by definition they are not aspirational.

Objectivity is not defined in ICD 203. The dictionary definition of objectivity is ‘the quality of being objective’ with ‘objective’ then defined as: ‘pertinent to the object; relating to that which is external to the mind; unbiased.’⁵ Meanwhile, the connotations associated with objectivity are widely varied. Objectivity can mean different things to different people, such as procedural rigor, independence, departmental or policy neutrality, honesty, or integrity, to include variants of either absolute absence of bias (being unbiased) or absolute truth. But the meaning of objectivity in ICD 203 is not clear because no definition is provided.

The analytic objectivity standard in ICD 203 is different from all the other analytic standards in that it is written to apply specifically to the individual analyst and his or her processes rather than the analytic product. In 2007, ICD 203 specified that analysts and managers were required to ‘perform their analytic and informational functions from an unbiased perspective.’⁶ It went on to say that ‘analysis should be free of emotional content, give due regard to alternative perspectives and contrary reporting, and acknowledge developments that necessitate adjustments to analytic judgments.’ By 2015, this reference to the requirement that analysis be unbiased had been eliminated, and instead was replaced by a reference to how ‘analysts must perform their functions with objectivity and with awareness of their own assumptions and reasoning.’⁷ ICD 203 then says that analysts ‘must employ reasoning techniques and practical mechanisms that reveal and mitigate bias. Analysts should be alert to influence by existing analytic positions or judgments and must consider alternative perspectives and contrary information. Analysis should not be unduly constrained by previous judgments when new developments indicate a modification is necessary.’ In the end, it appears that this part of the standard is the only one that applies to analysts at the individual level as well as their practices and processes, but contrary to the guidance provided in ICD 203 is not applied to the finished analytic project itself.

Finally, analytic objectivity is different from analytic independence. ICD 203 distinguishes objectivity from independent of political considerations, even though the rationale and constructs for the two standards overlap. The 2007 version of ICD 203 acknowledged that overlap by suggesting that to achieve the standard of being independent of political consideration ‘analysts and managers should provide objective assessments informed by available information that are not distorted or altered with the intent of supporting or advocating a particular policy, political view point, or audience.’⁸ The 2015 version of ICD 203 abandoned reference to objectivity in favor of emphasizing that ‘Analytic assessments must not be distorted by, nor shaped for, advocacy of a particular audience, agenda, or policy viewpoint. Analytic judgments must not be influenced by the force of preference for a particular policy.’⁹ By splitting the two standards from each other, that has the effect of orienting ICD 203’s ‘objectivity’ standard more in the direction of its absolute value (truth) rather than its relative value (more objective than policy) which has significance for how objectivity as a standard is conceptualized.

There is historical path dependence to the development of objectivity as an analytic standard. ICD 203 is only the most recent iteration of analytic standards in the US IC. According to James Marchio, of the twelve principles for intelligence advocated by then DCI George Bush in 1976, one said that ‘total objectivity is the hallmark of all intelligence reports and estimates’ and another said that the IC’s ‘role is to provide information and professional judgments on foreign developments, without coloration by policy considerations.’¹⁰ These approximate the differentiation in ICD 203’s two standards of analytic objectivity and independence from political considerations. In addition, as Marchio observes, additional development of analytic standards were articulated in the 1980s through Helene Boatner’s articulation of standards for product evaluation.¹¹ Marchio then quotes Boatner as saying that ‘objectivity is the characteristic that separates intelligence analysis from advocacy or from catering to the policy preferences of our customers.’ These are snapshots of an ongoing conversation about intelligence analysis which has its origin decades earlier, in the 1940s during the debate over the creation of intelligence analysis as a function of government.

# History of analytic objectivity in the US IC

Analytic objectivity was originally seen as a way to increase both accuracy and influence. Sherman Kent and other early proponents of intelligence analysis were empirically oriented, data driven, primarily inductive in approach, with a strongly positivist orientation to the development of knowledge. Their belief was that the knowledge created through the accumulation and evaluation of data would provide a solid foundation for decision making.

In 1949 Sherman Kent said that it was important for intelligence analysts to know the ‘probable objective situation’ of other countries, with ‘objective situation’ defined as ‘the situation as it exists in the understanding of some hypothetical omniscient Being . . . .the situation stripped of the characteristics with which a prejudiced human observer is almost certain to endow it.’¹² To obtain that knowledge of the ‘objective situation,’ Kent says: ‘We insist, and have insisted for generations, that truth is to be approached, if not attained, through research guided by a systematic method. In the social sciences which very largely constitute the subject matter of strategic intelligence, there is such a method.’¹³ Here Kent is referring to the scientific method, as part of the pursuit of absolute truth. Accordingly, Kent says intelligence analysts ‘must be objective and impartial’ in the ‘selection of hypotheses’ . . . .and they must have ‘the capacity for . . . impartial objective analysis.’¹⁴

This linkage between science and objectivity is not coincidental. At the time – in the 1940s – there was great optimism about the prospects of turning social science into a science, and then using the results of that science to improve the human condition. This vision for using the scientific method to develop knowledge was embedded in the core of intelligence analysis from the very beginning. As Washington Platt observed in the mid-1950s, ‘there has been a great drive towards objectivity and quantification in the social sciences; and towards the separation of opinion and moral judgments from objective facts. The hope has been expressed that some day our knowledge of the laws of the social sciences would be comparable to our knowledge of the laws of the natural sciences.’¹⁵ Intelligence analysts, with access to data, and through the employment of reason and the scientific method, would be more likely to develop knowledge which would lead to better decisions, with the supporting norms reflecting that vision. So intelligence analysts took on aspects of the ethos of the scientist: the neutral observer, independent from outcomes, and as objective as possible as a way to get closer to ‘truth.’

But the primary value of objectivity was originally not perceived in absolute terms. Instead, its value was interpreted as relative; the degree to which it could improve the understandings of decision makers. Most of Kent’s references to objectivity are in his chapter on the relationship between intelligence producers and consumers. His primary concern is about how proximity between the two can threaten objectivity, and the policy failures that could result, and that is where the value of independence comes in.

Kent insisted on the independence, or separation, of intelligence analysis from policy to ensure that policy would be provided with the inconvenient fact or unwanted interpretation which was acquired through greater objectivity. In the 1940s, decision makers, with their departmental views, were seen as possessing both motivated and institutional biases, and they would see in the intelligence what they wanted to see, ignoring the rest, which could cause policy failures. So Sherman Kent wanted to separate intelligence analysts from policymakers through independence and autonomy to ensure that intelligence analysts would call the policymaker’s ‘attention to the stubborn fact they may be neglecting.’¹⁶ Or, as Roger Hilsman put it a few years later, it was to ‘guarantee that the policy (maker) will have to face the unpleasant facts that do not support his policy.’¹⁷ Once faced with the inconvenient fact or the unwanted interpretation – which were assumed to be closer to truth–the expectation was that decision makers would appreciate this, and then change their policies in accordance with this more objective assessment.

That is why both ‘objectivity’ and ‘independence’ are viewed as crucial in the ethos of the intelligence analyst. Objectivity is part of the search for truth with its value being absolute (closer to truth, more likely to be accurate) while independence – the separation of intelligence analysts from policymakers–ensures that the search for truth can continue unimpeded, with its value being relative (more objective than policy). These two characteristics combined to provide the rationale for the concept of ‘speaking truth to power’ as the aspirational goal of the intelligence analyst. According to then-Director of Central Intelligence Robert Gates, ‘Truth, insofar as we can determine it, is what our work is all about.’¹⁸ For analysts, the mechanism for achieving truth is analytic objectivity.

Possessing greater likelihood of increased accuracy through objectivity, intelligence analysts would provide the information and interpretations that policymakers did not want to hear. In that sense – and contrary to many current formulations – the original purpose of intelligence analysis was to increase uncertainty through this de facto challenge function rather than to decrease it. This is the model that formed the core set of expectations about the role and function of intelligence analysis vis-à-vis policy.

This way of framing the relationship between knowledge and action – which has been characterized as the ‘standard model’ of the role of intelligence analysis in decision making – has become embedded in the ethos of national security intelligence analysts.¹⁹ As such, analysts conceive of themselves as independent and objective experts, providing information and assessment about the international environment or other actors without engaging in the creation or implementation of policy. Analysts believe their purpose is to interpret the raw intelligence, determine its meaning and significance, evaluate it, develop estimates or forecasts of what is likely to happen next, and provide these expert judgments to national security decision makers in order to support – and optimally influence–their decision making processes, with objectivity and independence as their unique value added.²⁰ Or so goes the theory based on the standard model. But there is a noted difference between theory and practice.

# Analytic objectivity is not attainable

Studies of the history of intelligence analysis in the United States show that analytic objectivity has been impossible to achieve in practice. An early challenge came from analysts in the Research and Analysis unit of the Office of Strategic Services (OSS) during World War II. According to Barry Katz’s history of the unit, they questioned the ‘epistemological naivete’ of those who thought ‘analytic objectivity’ was achievable or even desirable.²¹ Katz says that ‘a significant faction’ of analysts in OSS ‘believing that data do not exist outside of an interpretive framework, resisted the empiricist assumption that the only path to the Holy Grail of objectivity lay in ordering supposedly raw data into an accessible and readily usable form.’ Katz then describes how analysts had running battles over applied epistemology through World War II, with some of them continuing to challenge these positivist frameworks after they went back into universities when the war ended.

Even Sherman Kent recognized that analytic objectivity was aspirational rather than achievable. He said ‘whereas knowledge of the objective situation is of highest desireability, any non-omniscient Being (ie. any frail human being) probably can never apprehend the true objective fact.’²² Kent also said ‘Faced with the necessity to provide objective and factual descriptions . . . intelligence simply cannot deliver.’²³ In the decades since, others have observed how objectivity is pursued but impossible to achieve in practice such as Thomas Hughes, former director of State Department’s Bureau of Intelligence and Research, and his reference to the ‘struggle for that elusive will-o’-thewisp called objectivity.’²⁴ Since then, many others have extended that challenge to objectivity methodologically, epistemologically, and philosophically.

In intelligence analysis, objectivity per ICD 203 is achieved at the level of the individual analyst by the effort to eliminate subjectivity by identifying biases and mitigating their effects. In that sense, it is a procedural form of objectivity consistent with University of Virginia professor Alan Megill’s observation that objectivity in a procedural sense ‘aims at the practice of an impersonal method of investigation’ in order to better represent ‘things as they really are.’²⁵

But it is impossible to eliminate bias altogether at the level of the individual analyst. Even if analysts try to be ‘objective’ in a procedural sense, they will not be able to achieve absolute objectivity because biases consisting of cognitive frameworks are necessary in order to infer meaning from incomplete data. Conceptual frameworks provide each analyst with a different kind of filter, for both understanding and interpretation, and a corresponding set of biases. As Jack Davis has said, ‘the analyst’s long-held standard of analytic objectivity has helped to promote an institutional ethic of pursing independence from all biases, including policy and political influences, in making judgments in the face of substantive uncertainty. But studies, including those commissioned by the (Central Intelligence) Agency, indicate that substantive biases (experience-based mindsets) are all but essential for effectiveness in an environment of high-volume production and tight deadlines.’²⁶

In fact, a CIA report based on a seminar on bias in intelligence analysis acknowledged that bias is likely to be inevitable in intelligence analysis. According to the report, several participants in the seminar ‘asserted that it was impossible to produce finished intelligence that did not display some bias. The outside influences of home, family, religion, ethnic group, and education are at work on any individual and cannot help but exert themselves to a greater or lesser degree upon the individual analyst.’²⁷ This CIA report goes on to say that ‘there is probably no way to overcome (biases), except to strive consciously for as much objectivity as possible.’²⁸

So bias at the individual level is impossible to avoid, and those are only a subset of the kinds of biases affecting intelligence analysts. There are also motivated biases, political biases, policy biases, cognitive biases, and so on. Other kinds of biases also exist at different levels of analysis. According to the CIA report, embedded in intelligence analysis are unit, organizational, departmental, and institutional biases. These biases have been of significant concern for senior managers of intelligence organizations for decades.²⁹

Complicating matters further is that a bias may be embedded into the core function of intelligence analysis itself. The goal of ensuring that decision makers are faced with the inconvenient fact and unwanted interpretation requires a bias in the direction of warning. This has been described in the literature as a kind of pessimism that is balanced against the policymakers’ optimism. At its best, this bias alerts decision makers to the negative situations or outcomes they have not yet been giving their full attention to. At its worst, this bias can manifest itself as the over-emphasis of possible negative outcomes, risking what Roger Hilsman described as analysts becoming ‘warning critics’ (implicitly criticizing policy) or what Robert Gates later described as ‘poking an analytical finger in the policy eye.’³⁰ Policymakers sometimes interpret this as biased intelligence analysis.³¹ This bias to warn – which can produce distortions in terms of representing things as they really are – seems to be intrinsic to the purpose and value of intelligence analysis itself.

This bias towards warning can also mean that interpretations that are closer to what policymakers want to hear or expect to hear, which are assumed to be more subjective, can turn out to be more accurate because intelligence analysts may have put their intellectual thumb on the scale in terms of ensuring warning. One example of this may be the case of the order-of-battle dispute during the Vietnam War. In this situation, military intelligence assessments were more optimistic and the CIA’s assessments were more pessimistic, with the more optimistic assessments incorporated into the National Intelligence Estimate on the subject. Sam Adams was the CIA analyst, and complained about analytic politicization after the fact.³² In a later evaluation, James Wirtz argued that in this case – while both assessments were wrong – the CIA assessments were more wrong.³³ This raises a significant issue, which is that intelligence analysis which is more independent and objective is not necessarily more correct than departmental analysis.

Since all intelligence analysis involves at least some degree of bias and therefore subjectivity, in practice, ‘analytic objectivity’ defined in absolute terms – meaning the universality of perception, elimination of all subjectivity, true impartiality, absolute reflection of truth without bias or distortion, and a mechanism for achieving absolute accuracy – is an unattainable ideal.

This also significantly complicates efforts to identify analytic politicization, which is a violation of the norm or standard of objectivity. If all analysis entails to one degree or another subjectivity, bias and distortion, then trying to operationalize ‘analytic politicization’–or the inappropriate distortion of the analytic product – is similarly problematic. The problem with the concept of analytic politicization is that when there are differences of opinion between analysts, those analysts whose position is closer to what policymakers want to hear are not necessarily distorting their analysis.³⁴ Instead, sometimes the positions that are closer to what policymakers want to hear are actually more accurate in an absolute sense than independent analysis. Identifying incidents of analytic politicization effectively requires shifting the orientation away from diagnosing distortion or bias, which is going to be there regardless, and more in the direction of intent, which can be incredibly difficult. The bottom line is that efforts to achieve objectivity do not necessarily lead one closer to truth, leaving ‘truth’ – as implied in the phrase ‘speaking truth to power’–both a complicated and contested space.

# Analytic objectivity decrease relevance and influence

But even if analytic objectivity were attainable, it might not have as much positive value as initially thought. Intelligence analysis was created as a separate function of government to enable it to provide the inconvenient fact and unwanted interpretation so that policymakers could change their policies and prevent failures. But after decades of experience we now know that the *early expectations of significant analytic influence were overly optimistic*. As Thomas Fingar observed, ‘Greater objectivity, actual or assumed, does not necessarily make IC contributions more influential.’³⁵ History shows that in cases when policymakers are provided with the *inconvenient fact and unwanted interpretation*, this analysis was not used to change policy but instead – according to Robert Gates– *usually ignored*.³⁶

***Decisionmaking is frequently necessarily subjective***, driven by concepts, values, and other normative judgments which shape the interpretation of what is or could be. Decisionmakers are frequently faced with multiple versions of the ‘truth.’ It is *the decisionmaker’s conceptual framework, or mindset, or theories, which provides the rationale for choosing one explanation over another*, and the final analyst of last resort is always on the policy side. According to Richard Horowitz, ‘political decisionmakers often have concerns beyond the realm of intelligence. *The function of the top political leadership is to protect and advance a country’s interests*, and in its view, deciding against certain “truths” may be a necessary step in so doing.’³⁷

In addition, the bias in favor of warning means that intelligence analysis is not necessarily more accurate than policy analysis. At times policymakers will appropriately discount or disregard these warnings, as acting on them would lead to very expensive policy failures. This kind of *correct rejection of inconvenient intelligence analysis* is only infrequently captured in the literature, yet *provides a significant counterpoint to the charge that ignoring intelligence analysis produces policy failure*.³⁸ While specific instances can be identified where policymaker disregard of intelligence analysis led to policy failures, there are many other instances in which policymakers rejected intelligence analysis warnings which – if acted upon – would have led to other kinds of policy failures. The *rejection of the inconvenient fact or unwanted interpretation in and of itself is not evidence of a flawed decisionmaking process* precisely because of the presence of this **embedded bias to warn**.

Combined, this poses a direct challenge to the initial value proposition for the pursuit of objectivity in the first place. If policymakers, per Robert Gates’ observation, usually ignore the inconvenient fact or unwanted interpretation, ***the more intelligence analysts pursue objectivity the less relevant or influential their analysis may be***.

# Analytic objectivity embedded in idealistic myths and slogans

Analytic objectivity may in fact represent a heroic, idealized myth regarding the role of the intelligence analyst in the policy process. According to a CIA article from the mid-1970s, the assumption that intelligence analysis would provide the primary foundation for policy and decision was inaccurate because ‘*national security policy is not directly driven by facts, analyses, and judgments, and knowledge does not lead to action in any simple way*. What also come into play are *competing bureaucratic pressures*, a wide range of *domestic considerations*, the concepts, perceptions and stereotypes held by the *policy makers themselves*, and a host of essentially political inputs.’³⁹ But, as the article goes on to say, this **assumption** ‘has been *hard to shake*, so firmly has it become *part of the mythology*.’

This word ‘mythology’ provides a useful way to frame the concept of analytic objectivity, because it too may be a myth. In fact, according to a different CIA report, one prevalent ‘myth’ is that intelligence analysts ‘always approach every problem of analysis from perfect objectivity.’⁴⁰ That report then identified an ‘*anti-bias bias’* possessed by intelligence analysts which represents the belief that ‘*we are really the only people who can look objectively upon the issue at hand and come up with a soundly reasoned statement assessing the situation dispassionately*. We place high premiums upon objectivity and grant perhaps too much lip service to the degree of it which we attain.’ This anti-bias bias can be seen in Thomas Fingar’s writings, in which he says that all ‘other contributors to the (policy) process . . . have a preferred policy outcome’ and will ‘construct their arguments and analyses to support that preference,’ with the contribution of intelligence analysts more objective by definition.⁴¹ Perhaps it is from this anti-bias bias that the myth of the objective intelligence analyst is born.

These references to the mythology of intelligence analysis are the byproduct of working intelligence analysts reflecting on their own professional identities. In these cases the intelligence analysts recognize that embedded in these professional identities may be characteristics that are functionally heroic or idealized rather than descriptively accurate regarding practices, processes, and contributions. As Douglas MacEachin pointed out, ‘Objectivity is not a matter of who makes the judgment; analysis is not objective simply because it originates from an intelligence analyst, any more than it is automatically nonobjective if it originates from a policy official. *Bias is not the private preserve of anyone*. Analysts can become wedded to a certain analytic view, just as policymakers can become wedded to a policy view.’⁴² Yet despite this acknowledgement, there is still a desire to represent intelligence analysis as if the mythology were achievable in practice.

The slogan that represents the ‘*heroic, idealized myth’* of the *objective intelligence analyst is ‘Speaking Truth to Power*.’ As Robert Gates has said, ‘Our deepest commitment . . . is to speak truth to power.’⁴³ Also, according to the ODNI’s ‘Principles of Professional Ethics for the Intelligence Community,’ ‘We seek the truth; speak truth to power; and obtain, analyze, and provide intelligence objectively.’⁴⁴ This slogan is powerful, unambiguous, and says something significant about the role and function of intelligence analysis. At the same time, it also *represents an unrealistic, heroically mythic role for intelligence analysts*.

Some intelligence professionals have challenged the value of this slogan. As Mark Lowenthal has said: the search for truth is ‘a nice sentiment, but it overstates and misrepresents what is going on . . . . Intelligence is not about truth . . . Truth is such an absolute term that it sets a standard that intelligence rarely would be able to achieve.’⁴⁵ Or as ***Joseph Wippl*** has said, ‘the issue is not about speaking “truth to power,” having the connotation of “I have the truth and you do not,” but rather *presenting the facts to power as best as humans know them, defined by time and place*.’⁴⁶ Another challenge has come from ***Jennifer Sims***: ‘The notion that intelligence holds “the truth” (and policymakers do not) undermines the process of intelligence support.’⁴⁷ She goes on to say that if analysts are “Imbued with the ethic of ‘speaking truth to power’ they ‘may forget that *power and truth exist on both sides of the analyst-customer divide* and that some truths – although objective and seemingly relevant – are nonetheless distinctly unhelpful.’⁴⁸

Jack Davis also agrees that the slogans which embody heroic ideals are problematic. As he has written, ‘the conceit that (intelligence analysis is) free of policy and political influence or bias comes across in the slogans by which the analysts and their leaders usually define their professional mission: objective analysis, carrying truth to power, telling it like it is.’⁴⁹ Instead, Davis recommends developing a ‘more realistic standard’ along the lines of ‘tell it like they THINK it is.’⁵⁰ That kind of slogan is functionally the equivalent to ‘calling it as you see it’ with both having at their core the characteristics of honesty, integrity, and courage which are also heroic in their own way. In that sense, this slogan is the embodiment of the best of the value of independence in the same way that ‘speaking truth to power’ is the embodiment of the unattainable ideal of objectivity.

These competing slogans are a manifestation of a ***tension** associated with trying to develop relevant **knowledge objectively** while simultaneously supporting **policy developed subjectively***. This tension has existed through the entire history of intelligence analysis and – according to former National Security Advisor Brent Scowcroft in 2009–has never been fully resolved.⁵¹ If policymakers usually ignore the inconvenient fact and unwanted interpretation then the value of objectivity in intelligence analysis may be less than Sherman Kent and others originally thought.

# Analytic objectivity derived from idealized version of science

Intelligence analysts’ embrace of analytic objectivity was been drawn from idealized conceptualizations of the relationship between information, knowledge, and decision that existed decades ago, with an emphasis on the scientific method as a kind of value-neutral epistemological framework used to develop knowledge ‘objectively.’ Since the 1940s, Sherman Kent and others have argued that intelligence analysis could be more effective if it were framed as a social science with the scientific method at its core.⁵² The degree to which social scientific approaches were historically embedded in analytic practice was scattershot until Robert Gates’ efforts to improve intelligence analysis in the early 1990s and the downstream establishment of formal analytic tradecraft, tradecraft standards, and structured analytic techniques modeled on social scientific practices.⁵³ But this embrace of analytic objectivity within the broader practices of scientific inquiry has come under significant challenge in the decades since, and provides a poor foundation for contemporary conceptualizations of the applied epistemology of intelligence analysis, as well as performance standards.

While many intelligence scholars address the importance of an ‘objective’ contribution to national security decision making as if it were unique to intelligence, in fact it is not.⁵⁴ For example, historians have been debating the nature of objectivity for decades, if not centuries.⁵⁵ Science, too, has also traditionally pursued knowledge ‘objectively.’ According to Bruce Bimber, a professor at the University of California-Santa Barbara, the ‘idealized image of the scientific expert involves not simply knowledge, but also a large element of objectivity, of being above politics and partisanship.

The idealized policy expert brings the neutral authority of science to bear on politics. Experts derive legitimacy from their ability to appeal to non-political professional standards (including) the use of dispassionate scientific methods of inquiry.’⁵⁶ Bimber points out that during World War II, ‘many had come to think of experts as an apolitical corps that could be trusted to help defeat the enemies’ and that when the war ended, it seemed particularly important to create mechanisms for specialized expertise to flow into public policy discussions. It was in this environment that a peacetime intelligence function independent of policy departments was created to facilitate an independent, objective flow of information into the national security decisionmaking process. A comparison between intelligence analysts and experts such as scientists, social scientists, and other kinds of analysts who support the public policy process can be established based on the similarity of role and function they play.

Like intelligence analysts, the public policy analyst is an advisor rather than a creator or implementer of policy, providing knowledge for the betterment of decision, policy, and society. Some public policy analysts reside within government bureaucracies to provide decision makers with the knowledge needed to evaluate policy options effectively. Accordingly, intelligence analysts can be conceptualized as a kind of public policy analyst, with specialized knowledge and expertise, providing their neutral, independent, and nominally ‘objective’ input into an inherently political policy process. According to Glenn Hastedt – a professor at James Madison University–‘the professional is an intermediary between abstract knowledge and political action. He or she translates that information into a form which is both understandable and usable by the non-expert. Deference to the professional typically rests upon the presumption that compromise and negotiation, the hallmarks of political deliberation, have no significant impact on the manner by which professionals arrive at their conclusions. Rationality and objectivity are held to guide their analysis of information.’⁵⁷

The importance of rationality and objectivity is most applicable in the case of the scientific expert supporting the policymaking process. As Kathleen Vogel has put it, ‘scientific knowledge is seen as objective and value free, and scientists are believed to operate under norms of disinterestedness; they do not seek personal gain, but act for the benefit of the public and scientific community. According to this perspective, the scientific expert’s job is to provide technical input to policy problems, while remaining independent of political influences.’⁵⁸ Normative expectations about the role of the analyst are similar. For example in science there is a version of the standard model that presumes an idealized, formalistic process for the integration of knowledge into decision making. In science, according to University of Colorado’s Roger Pielke, a notion sometimes referred to as the ‘linear model’ conceptualizes the sequence of policymaking as first establishing the scientific facts which then lead to a policy response.⁵⁹ As Pielke goes on to say, ‘under the linear model, science supposedly matters because it dictates which policies make sense and which do not.’

But the linear model in science has similar flaws to the standard model in intelligence analysis. Contrary to the expectation drawn from the linear model that policymaking will be based on an expert analysis of the data, frequently policymaking hinges on the balancing of values necessary to manage a problem rather than expert analysis regarding what can be done to solve it.⁶⁰ Public policy analysts also face problems similar to intelligence analysts in the integration and acceptance of their expertise into the policymaking process. As Robert Hoppe observes, ‘the relationship between political judgment and science-based expertise is a troubled one,’⁶¹ which approximates the troubled relationship between intelligence analysts and policymaking that Sherman Kent described in 1949.⁶² Harvard University’s Mark Moore illustrates these problems and their effects in the context of evaluating the utility of social science research for policymaking.⁶³ As he says, some tensions have developed in the relationship, producing disillusionment on both sides. According to Moore, ‘the policymakers are increasingly frustrated by the inability of social scientists to produce compelling information in the form they need at the time they need it and are tired of being accused of acting in ignorance by any expert whose advice was ignored. For their part, the social scientists feel that their scientific virtue is under constant attack and that their important findings and cautions are cavalierly brushed aside by policymakers. So a certain prickliness has appeared in what promised to be a friendly relationship.’ A very similar prickliness exists in the relationship between intelligence analysts and decision makers as well.

In addition, the myths and slogans in science and intelligence analysis are similar as well, as are the assumptions that underlie them. The social scientific and scientific literatures demonstrate that the notion of ‘speaking truth to power’ in the intelligence analysis context is derived from the same notion existing in these larger and broader domains.⁶⁴ Similarly, the term politicization is used when politics appears to interfere with what the experts believe to be truth in other kinds of public policymaking as well. As Columbia University professor Jeffrey Henig put it in context of education policy, distinctions must be made ‘between politics as a corrupting force and politics as a healthy and necessary component of democratic decision making, and between politicization that permeates the research itself and politicization of the ways in which research is communicated to the policy world.’⁶⁵ In making these distinctions, Henig points out that some believe policy ‘ought to be made in an arena from which politics has been banished. This has been referenced in such terms as the apolitical myth and antipolitics. This idealized vision of the relationship between research and politics portrays a temporally and functionally delimited role for democratic politics.’⁶⁶ This ‘apolitical myth’ is embedded in many of the definitions of politicization in the intelligence context as well.

Over time, the scientific community has been re-evaluating the role of scientific objectivity in public policy decision making and by the 1960s and 1970s had transformed the scientific understanding of the role of the expert. According to Bimber, the ‘naiveté behind that idealized image of technical experts . . . eroded in the face of policy debates that revealed political fault lines within the scientific community . . . .making abundantly clear the point that scientists, engineers, and other experts on public issues had values and political interests that shaped their ostensibly neutral, technical contributions to politics.’⁶⁷ As Bimber notes, today ‘few observers believe any longer that this ideal is often realized in practice.’

Perhaps the scientific method and the ethos of the scientist provide a poor foundation for contemporary conceptualizations of the applied epistemology of intelligence analysis, as well as expectations about its role, function, and contributions. As Richard Betts has observed, the strict separation between intelligence analysis and decisionmaking may be ‘an unrealistic application of “pure science” norms to areas where fact and value are inseparable and judgment must preside.’⁶⁸ Studying the role and contribution of the expert analyst in the public policy process may help us understand, explain, and potentially improve upon what happens at the intersection of intelligence, policy, and politics.

# Learning from other kinds of public policy analysts

Expert analysts play a limited role in public policy more generally because it is very difficult to resolve conflicts over truth at the intersection of fact and value. When politicization is defined as a corruption of the message in either intelligence analysis or public policy analysis, it is built on an assumption that policy should defer to the subject matter knowledge and expertise of the expert analyst and advisor. The underlying assumption that knowledge, expertise, or science should lead to a policy response is essentially no different from the idealized standard model of the intersection of analysis and decision that predominates in both intelligence practice and scholarship. In this model, intelligence analysis is seen as the best interpretation of fact and serves as a precursor to and foundation for policy; an idealized vision infrequently upheld in practice.

But the assumptions that underlie the standard model are just as inaccurate as those that prevail in the broader public policy literatures. The intersection of expert knowledge, policy and politics is more complicated than these simple assumptions can account for, and instead the problem may have more to do with inappropriate expectations that knowledge or expertise should convert directly to decision making. To address these expectations, Henig suggests that rather than engage in the effort to keep production of expert knowledge protected from the pressures of policy and politics – which Pielke suggests would be ‘doomed to fail’ – the goal should be ‘to improve the ways in which research is used in a political world.’⁶⁹ But how exactly should that be done?

Maximizing influence requires integrating intelligence analysis into a subjective decision making process but it is not clear whether intelligence analysts are supposed to embrace the values of the social scientist–independence, objectivity and pursuit of truth–or the instrumental values of the policy analyst. Most of the time there is a functional differentiation between these two roles, and friction inevitably develops. According to Mark Moore, ‘The prickliness we observe exists because the professional virtues of social scientists fail to mesh neatly with the task of policy analysis, and this leads to disappointment and mutual suspicion on both sides.’⁷⁰

The problem for intelligence analysts is that they are expected to perform both roles simultaneously. In the broader context, according to Mark Moore, the social sciences will be at arm’s length from government as a way to achieve autonomy and independence while policy analysts are ‘inevitably closely intertwined with governmental actions and concerns. In fact . . . the closer the relationship, the better.’⁷¹ But the tradeoffs are the same as in the intelligence context as well. Greater proximity risks loss of intellectual integrity, while greater distance risks loss of influence.⁷² The solution, according to Moore, is for policy analysts to ‘develop a professional norm resistant to such pressures.’ The development of much the same kind of norm exists in intelligence analysis as well.

In this differentiation between social science and policy analysts, intelligence analysts appear closer to the latter than the former. Yet part of the problem is that they retain some of the culture and objectives of the social scientist. They sometimes seek truth rather than useful knowledge. They sometimes view policy as derivative of knowledge rather than politics. They sometimes believe that knowledge rather than politics is what does – or should – drive policy. Perhaps there are inherent tensions in this hybrid role which complicate the role that the expert intelligence analyst plays in supporting national security policymaking. Or perhaps there are other kinds of experts in government who have a similarly hybrid role.

There is potential value in extending this comparison to other kinds of public policy analysts since what happens at the intersection of intelligence and policy may in the end be no different from what happens anywhere else in government where expert analysts fact challenges in their support of the public policymaking process.

# Preliminary conclusions

After looking at this history of intelligence analysis, here are some *tentative* conclusions:

First, it is *not clear what the US IC means by analytic objectivity* or what the provision in ICD 203 is intended to accomplish. Based on the experiences documented in the intelligence studies literature it seems like ***objectivity as a standard is highly problematic given its orientation to the absolute***. While aspirations to analytic objectivity are a noble ideal, it ***cannot be achieved in practice***. Because of that, if objectivity is the standard, and all ‘analysts must perform their function with objectivity’ then all analysts will be deficient in achieving that standard. On the other hand, if the purpose of the standard is to establish objectivity as an aspiration, perhaps that aspiration would be better located in a mission statement, a code of ethics, or other reflections of professional ethos rather than standards. Essentially, it might make sense to shift the ethos away from absolutes – like ‘seeking truth’ or ‘speak truth to power’ – and instead *embrace more relative considerations like honesty, integrity, and ‘call it as you see it*.’ Perhaps *pursuit of objectivity* should be the standard rather than the attainment of it.

Second, rather than establish processes for pursuing analytic objectivity at the level of the individual analyst as ICD 203 does, it ***may be possible to more comprehensively pursue analytic objectivity through formal organizational procedures and processes***. For example, in the mid-1990s, Doug ***MacEachin*** – in developing the core principles underlying analytic tradecraft – suggested that analytic *objectivity was a byproduct of the principles and processes of tradecraft in the aggregate*, ‘as the means that help all of us avoid falling victim to our own perspectives.’⁷³ MacEachin suggested that ‘the ***test o***f ***objectivity*** is *how well the evidence is laid out* and *its reliability exposed*, and how *openly and carefully* the basis for any conclusion is *presented*.’ However, the procedural approach to developing analytic objectivity is presented in the 2015 version of ICD 203 at the individual level rather than the organizational process level, which is not how MacEachin conceptualized it when he developed the concept of analytic tradecraft.⁷⁴ *For MacEachin*, **objectivity is a byproduct of the aggregate processes associated with all aspects of analytic tradecraft**, not just those employed by individuals to identify and mitigate the effects of bias. For example, processes associated with the practice of coordination can be a means of identifying, challenging, and reducing the impact of subjectivity on analysis. As ***Heuer*** says, ‘objectivity is achieved by *exposing oneself to alternative viewpoints and making one’s basic assumptions and reasoning as open and explicit as possible* *so that they can be challenged by others*.’⁷⁵ In that sense, perhaps ICD203 has framed analytic objectivity too narrowly, and instead it should be framed as a byproduct of the other analytic tradecraft standards in the aggregate. Perhaps a procedural mechanism broader than just individual processes can be articulated for the pursuit of analytic objectivity.

Third, embracing the use of social scientific practices to help make the processes closer to those that approximate objectivity may continue to replicate in an intelligence context all of the myths and misunderstandings that used to prevail in science. There is also *no formally established version of applied epistemology for intelligence analysis* which provides a framework for analysts to go from information or *data*, to *knowledge*, to *insight*. If there is *a dominant form* of applied epistemology, it is ‘science’ as operationalized through *approximations of the scientific method*. In September 2016, the Office of the Undersecretary of Defense for Intelligence organized a symposium on Analytic Objectivity ‘for sharing research and methodologies to ensure unbiased, objective analysis and research.’⁷⁶ This conference identified science and the scientific method as a means by which analytic objectivity could be approximated. But science has significant flaws and limitations to its application in an intelligence analysis context.⁷⁷ These limitations are so significant that the adoption of best practices based on the scientific method is currently vulnerable to the charges that it is more of a pseudo-science intended to borrow the perceived legitimacy of science rather than achieve actual improvement in practice. For example, as has been highlighted in the ‘intelligence analysis as science or art’ debate, it is not clear that if the scientific method is applied to non-representative samples, that the end result is reliable, validated knowledge that would equate to ‘science.’

Similarly, even though principles of the scientific method are embedded in Structured Analytic Techniques, it is not clear that their use makes the end result science, or objective, especially since subjective judgments lie at the core of many of the techniques. As a team of researchers recently argued, *even structured analytic techniques ‘rely upon subjective analytic inputs’* and ‘may serve only as a vehicle transporting subjectivity from one end of the process to the other. The SAT process becomes an end in itself, dressing up subjective judgments in a cloak of objectivity.’⁷⁸ In the absence of an agreed epistemology for intelligence analysis, some effort must be devoted to ensuring that the practices which are implemented are not just borrowing the perceived legitimacy associated with science, but instead are justified using some other kind of criteria such as accuracy, if that can even be approximated and measured.

Fourth, there may also be value in integrating intelligence analysis into a subjective decision making process by starting with the concepts of the decision maker, and then integrating the intelligence analysis into that, functionally merging the two. This is an alternative model for doing intelligence analysis which was advocated by Willmoore Kendall in 1949, Roger Hilsman in the 1950s, Thomas Hughes in the 1970s, and many others since then.⁷⁹ A historical example of how this can play out in practice is the way that the US IC worked with the National Security Council staff to produce National Security Studies Memorandums and Presidential Review Memorandums in the Nixon, Ford and Carter administrations.⁸⁰ These were very popular and influential products – seen as much more valuable from a top-line perspective than other forms of finished intelligence, including National Intelligence Estimates. This approach is also comparable to that of the British Joint Intelligence Committee, in which policymakers also developed joint appreciations with their intelligence colleagues.⁸¹ Recent calls for a modern day equivalent would – if created–provide a platform for this kind of intelligence and policy integration.⁸² Doing so would maximize the benefits of independence without the limitations associated with the pursuit of absolute objectivity, and perhaps increase relevance and influence.

Fifth, there may be other ways of knowing and understanding which do not pursue objectivity that may be worth exploring. Analytic objectivity is conceived of as a way to get closer to truth, but it has not been established that efforts to be objective lead one closer to truth, as truth is also contingent on value propositions that are inherently subjective. Nor is it clear that those who try to pursue more objective ways of getting to truth actually produce knowledge that is more valuable than more subjective ways of getting to truth. Maybe the historical vision for intelligence analysis that Sherman Kent articulated and has been maintained through the history of intelligence in the United States is incomplete in terms of getting to the best ways of knowing and understanding. Maybe there are other ways to get to different kinds of truths–or pursuing other kinds of values– which may be different from those which involve the pursuit of objectivity but at the same time worth exploring. For example, there may be value in embracing intersubjectivity as a ‘pragmatic surrogate’ for objectivity, as Mark Phythian and Pete Gill recommend.⁸³ But for the US IC to get there would require acknowledging the subjectivity inherent in intelligence analysis and developing mechanisms to address it.

Finally, there may also be value in approaches that abandon aspects of the current ethos of intelligence analysis altogether. For example, there is potential benefit in Nathan Woodard’s recommendation to harness rhetoric within an ethical framework to provide policymakers with the benefits of ‘positive politicization.’⁸⁴ Woodard distinguishes between objectivity and neutrality, and suggests that while objectivity is unattainable, striving for it is still useful whereas striving for neutrality is not. This is another way of orienting to a different set of values by pursuing objectivity, enhancing relevance, retaining honesty, but abandoning expectations of policy neutrality. Or perhaps there is value in exploring the potential benefit from Brian Holmes’ reflections on using ‘pathos’ or emotional appeal – within an ethical context–directly in intelligence analysis.⁸⁵

Each of these new approaches would directly challenge assumptions that were in play in the 1940s and are still embedded in the US IC’s myths, slogans, and ethos of intelligence analysts, as manifested in the standard of analytic objectivity. As Michael Herman has said, ‘Intelligence is part of the government system, and has to have empathy and credibility with the policy-makers it serves. It cannot adopt an Olympian objectivity, or detach itself completely from government’s policies and preconceptions. It has to sell its product, and has to be sensitive to its audience to do so.’⁸⁶ At the same time, this is not an argument for the abandonment of the search for truth in favor of assessments that support policy pronouncements or policies – such as those pursued by the Trump Administration – which do not seem to be based on any reasonable interpretation of fact. Instead, some of these new ideas may provide the US IC with the opportunity to continue to pursue truth, but through ways that produce knowledge which is more than the sum of the parts.
