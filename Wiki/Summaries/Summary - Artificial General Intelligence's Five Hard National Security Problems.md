## Executive Summary
Jim Mitre and Joel B. Predd of RAND argue that the emergence of [[Artificial General Intelligence|artificial general intelligence]] (AGI) is plausible and should be taken seriously by the U.S. national security community, though its pace and consequences are deeply uncertain.[^1] They contrast AI with the atomic bomb: frontier AI has not yet had an "atom-splitting moment" showing a clear path to a wonder weapon.[^2] The paper names five hard problems: wonder weapons, systemic shifts in power, nonexperts empowered to build weapons of mass destruction, artificial entities with agency, and instability.[^1] The authors offer the five as a common language and a rubric for judging strategies, because progress on one can undermine another.[^1] They assess current U.S. strategy as constructive but inadequate for a disruptive breakthrough.[^3]

## Main Points
- Uncertainty about scaling, thresholds, and timing is "endemic," so a strategy over-optimized for one future is high-risk.[^4]
- Each problem rests on different assumptions, from sudden breakthrough to gradual adoption.[^5]
- Relying heavily on compute and [[Semiconductor Export Controls|export controls]] could prove brittle.[^3]

## Themes
- Planning under uncertainty rather than prediction.
- Historical analogy: the Manhattan Project, the nuclear arms race, and the Industrial Revolution.

## Key Takeaways
- Explicit: develop high-regret policy options and contingency plans in advance, and run scenario exercises on post-AGI futures.[^3]
- [inferred] The authors want the five problems weighed together rather than one at a time.
