## Executive Summary
Louis Kaiser and Randolph Pherson's guide treats analytic writing as a step-by-step process. A paper's direction comes from five focal questions, taken in order: what has changed, why now, the impact so far, the outlook, and the implications for the reader ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 3 (scan).pdf#page=2|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 13]]). A detailed outline down to the paragraph level is part of the thinking, not only the writing ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 4 (scan).pdf#page=3|Kaiser & Pherson, pp. 21–22]]). Each paragraph rests on a topic sentence that makes an analytic judgment, supported by two to four pieces of evidence ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=3|Kaiser & Pherson, pp. 29, 49]]). Refining the draft means checking each paragraph's coherence, cutting words, and reviewing the whole as the reader would ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 9-11 (scan).pdf#page=2|Kaiser & Pherson, pp. 61–74]]).

## Themes
- Writing for a busy target audience, whose interests decide what the paper covers.
- Message-driven rather than data-driven organization: see [[Inverted Triangle]] ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 4 (scan).pdf#page=5|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 23]]).

## Key Takeaways
- Explicit: the most important requirement for a topic sentence in an analytic paper is that it includes analysis ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=3|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 29]]).
- [inferred] The guide treats clear writing and clear thinking as the same task.
