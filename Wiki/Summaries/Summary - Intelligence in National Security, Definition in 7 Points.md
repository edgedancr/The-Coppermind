## Executive Summary
This Intro to Intelligence handout defines intelligence in national security in seven points.[^1] It opens with Maureen Baginski's definition, "information about those who would do us harm," concerning plans, intentions, and capabilities.[^2] Following Mark Lowenthal, it says products must be tailored to scope, mission, and user, and outlines the intelligence process and the community's organization.[^3] Intelligence serves users from policymakers to tribal and international organizations.[^4] Point 6 names persistent themes and the moral principles intelligence requires.[^5] It closes with Treverton and Haver: intelligence is now in the information business as well as the secrets business.[^6]

## Main Points
- Intelligence aims to reduce uncertainty and estimate "proximate reality".[^2]
- Good intelligence is timely, tailored, digestible, and states what is known and unknown.[^7]
- Ethics are needed because of human nature's "inclination… to use power for selfish ends".[^5]

## Themes
- Intelligence as product, process, and organization.
- Old problems facing new actors and technologies.

## Key Takeaways
- Explicit: the Cold War's leftovers must give way to "new thinking, tools, process, and restructure".[^6]
- [inferred] The handout is meant as a compact definition students can reproduce.
