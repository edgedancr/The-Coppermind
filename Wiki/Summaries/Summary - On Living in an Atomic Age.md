## Executive Summary
In this 1948 essay, [[C. S. Lewis]] argues that we think "a great deal too much" of the atomic bomb.[^1] He says it adds only one more chance of death to a world where death was already certain.[^1] If the bomb comes, it should find us doing "sensible and human things," because it may break our bodies but need not dominate our minds.[^1] Lewis says the real question is whether Nature is the only thing that exists; if it is, civilization ends in nothing anyway.[^2] He then argues that [[Naturalism|naturalism]] undermines the very reasoning that tells us about Nature.[^3] His thesis: we are spirits who come from "another world," and must live by the law of love and temperance, never putting survival first.[^4]

## Main Points
- The bomb reminds us of the kind of world we live in, which prosperity before 1914 let us forget.[^2]
- If Nature is all, one might commit suicide, grab what pleasure one can, or defy the universe; Lewis argues the last two, which most materialists alternate between, both fail.[^5]
- Nature is our sister under a common Creator, not our mother.[^6]

## Themes
- Perspective on catastrophe: the new threat is not unique.
- Human reason and values as evidence of something beyond Nature.

## Key Takeaways
- Explicit: pull ourselves together, and live by honorable and merciful means even at the cost of survival,[^1].[^6]
- Explicit: those who care for something more than civilization are the likeliest to preserve it.[^6]
