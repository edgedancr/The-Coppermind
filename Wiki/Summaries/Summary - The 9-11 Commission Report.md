## Executive Summary
The final report of the National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States (2004) gives the commission's account of the September 11 attacks and the recommendations that flow from it.[^1] Its ten commissioners, five Republicans and five Democrats, presented it "without dissent".[^1] Its aim "has not been to assign individual blame" but to give the fullest possible account and identify lessons.[^2] Chapters 1 to 10 narrate the attacks, al-Qaeda's rise, and the U.S. government's response before and after.[^3] Chapter 11 finds that the attacks revealed four kinds of failure: in imagination, policy, capabilities, and management.[^4] Chapter 12 sets out what to do and chapter 13 how to organize the government to do it.[^5]

## Main Points
- **The enemy:** [[Al-Qaeda]], [[Usama Bin Ladin]], [[Khalid Sheikh Mohammed]], and the [[Planes Operation|planes operation]].[^6]
- **The day:** [[September 11 Attacks (2001)]], [[Air Defense on September 11 (2001)]], and [[Emergency Response on September 11 (2001)]].[^7]
- **Before 9/11:** [[Covert Action Authorities Against Bin Ladin]], the [[USS Cole Bombing (2000)]], the [[Millennium Alert (1999–2000)]], the [[Summer of Threat (2001)]], and [[Late Leads Before September 11 (2001)]].[^8]
- **The diagnosis:** [[Failures of Imagination, Policy, Capabilities, and Management]].[^9]
- **The recommendations:** a [[Global Strategy Against Islamist Terrorism]],[^10] and a [[Director of National Intelligence]], a [[National Counterterrorism Center]], [[Need to Share|sharing]], and stronger [[Congressional Oversight of Intelligence|congressional oversight]].[^11]

## Themes
- Fault lines within the government, "between foreign and domestic intelligence, and between and within agencies," and the problems of managing and sharing information.[^2]
- The "benefit and the handicap of hindsight" in judging what officials could have known.[^4]

## Key Takeaways
- The Commission calls for "a balanced strategy for the long haul," new ways for agencies to work together, and dramatic change in Congress to strengthen oversight.[^2]
