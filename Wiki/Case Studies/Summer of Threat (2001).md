> [!summary]
> In the spring and summer of 2001, terrorist threat reporting rose to its highest level since the millennium alert; DCI George Tenet told the 9/11 Commission that "the system was blinking red" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=272|9/11 Report, pp. 255, 259]]). The Commission found that ==the attacks "fell into the void between the foreign and domestic threats"==: agencies abroad acted on the warnings, while domestic agencies "never mobilized" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=280|9/11 Report, pp. 263, 265]]).

## What Happened
- **The drumbeat (spring):** reports to top officials warned that Bin Ladin was planning multiple operations, and in late May of a possible plot to hijack an aircraft or storm an embassy to free prisoners ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=272|9/11 Report, pp. 255–256]]).
- **The peak (June–July):** a late-June advisory warned of a high probability of near-term "spectacular" attacks; on June 28 Richard Clarke wrote that attack planning "had reached a crescendo" and that a major attack was likely in July ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=274|9/11 Report, p. 257]]). Most reports pointed overseas, to Saudi Arabia, Israel, Bahrain, Kuwait, Yemen, and possibly Rome ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=273|9/11 Report, p. 256]]).
- **Responses abroad:** U.S. forces in six countries went to the highest protection level, the Fifth Fleet left Bahrain, the embassy in Yemen closed, and disruption operations ran in 20 countries ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=274|9/11 Report, pp. 257–258]]).
- **The August 6 PDB:** "Bin Ladin Determined to Strike in US," the 36th [[President's Daily Brief]] item on Bin Ladin or al-Qaeda that year and the first devoted to an attack in the United States ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=277|9/11 Report, p. 260]]). The Commission found no further discussion among the President and his top advisers of a domestic al-Qaeda attack before 9/11 ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=279|9/11 Report, p. 262]]).

## What Went Wrong
- **Few specifics:** the threats "contained few specifics regarding time, place, method, or target," and most pointed overseas ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=279|9/11 Report, pp. 262–263]]).
- **The foreign-domestic void:** foreign agencies watched for foreign threats abroad, domestic agencies waited for evidence of sleeper cells, and "no one was looking for a foreign threat to domestic targets" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=280|9/11 Report, p. 263]]).
- **No direction at home:** domestic agencies had no "game plan," and no one told them to make one; at a July 5 briefing they were told not to send advisories to the field ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=275|9/11 Report, pp. 258, 264]]). The FBI's July 2 message said it had "no information indicating a credible threat of terrorist attack in the United States" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=275|9/11 Report, p. 258]]).
- **Aviation:** none of the FAA's summer security directives increased checkpoint or onboard security ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=281|9/11 Report, p. 264]]).

## Lessons
- In sum, "the domestic agencies never mobilized in response to the threat": borders were not hardened, transportation was not fortified, and the public was not warned ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=282|9/11 Report, p. 265]]).
