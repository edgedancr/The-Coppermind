> [!summary]
> On September 11, the response in New York rested mainly on private firms and local first responders, who coped with a catastrophe "for which they were unprepared in terms of both training and mindset" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=332|9/11 Report, p. 315]]). The 9/11 Commission found the evacuation a success for civilians below the impact zones ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=333|9/11 Report, p. 316]]), but that ==the Incident Command System "did not function to integrate awareness among agencies or to facilitate interagency response"== at the World Trade Center ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=338|9/11 Report, p. 321]]). The Pentagon response, by contrast, was "generally effective" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=331|9/11 Report, p. 314]]).

## What Happened
- **After 1993:** the first World Trade Center bombing exposed weak emergency preparedness, and evacuating the towers took more than four hours ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=297|9/11 Report, p. 280]]). The Port Authority then spent an initial $100 million on improvements, including its fire safety plan ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=297|9/11 Report, p. 280]]).
- **102 minutes:** from the North Tower's impact at 8:46 to its collapse at 10:28 ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=302|9/11 Report, p. 285]]). Evacuation time fell to under an hour for most civilians able to leave; of the 2,052 civilian dead whose workplaces could be located, 1,942 worked or had meetings at or above the impact zones ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=333|9/11 Report, p. 316]]).
- **The cost:** 2,973 people died, the largest loss of life from hostile attack on U.S. soil; the FDNY lost 343, the Port Authority police 37, and the NYPD 23 ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=328|9/11 Report, p. 311]]).
- **The Pentagon:** the Arlington County Fire Department served as incident commander, command was established at 9:41, and a partial collapse at 9:57 injured no first responder because the area had been evacuated ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=331|9/11 Report, pp. 314–315]]).

## What Went Wrong
- **911 calls:** the FDNY ordered both towers fully evacuated by 8:57, but operators and dispatchers were not told, and for the next hour often advised civilians not to self-evacuate ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=335|9/11 Report, p. 318]]).
- **The South Tower:** "No decision has been criticized more" than building personnel's decision not to evacuate it immediately after the North Tower was hit, though the Commission found the advice understandable in context ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=334|9/11 Report, p. 317]]).
- **Command and communications:** the FDNY could not coordinate the number of units it dispatched, radios worked poorly in the high-rise, and the FDNY, NYPD, and Port Authority had separate command posts and did not share critical information ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=336|9/11 Report, pp. 319–321]]). The Commission concluded radio failure "was not the primary cause" of the many firefighter deaths in the North Tower ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=340|9/11 Report, p. 323]]).
- **The Pentagon too:** its after-action report found problems with self-dispatching and communications, and the Commission inferred that such problems "will likely recur in any emergency of similar scale" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=332|9/11 Report, p. 315]]).

## Lessons
- The Commission credited the Pentagon's success to trust among responders, the [[Incident Command System]], and a regional approach ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=331|9/11 Report, p. 314]]), and recommended that agencies nationwide adopt ICS and [[Unified Command|unified command]] ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=414|9/11 Report, p. 397]]).
- Civilians are the "first" first responders; see [[Private-Sector Preparedness]] ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=334|9/11 Report, p. 317]]).
- For the military's improvised response the same morning, see [[Air Defense on September 11 (2001)]].
