> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission found that ==the defense of U.S. airspace on 9/11 "was not conducted in accord with preexisting training and protocols"==: it was improvised by civilians and a military unprepared for commercial aircraft used as weapons ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=48|9/11 Report, p. 31]]). The military's Northeast Air Defense Sector had nine minutes' notice of the first hijacked plane and none of the other three ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=48|9/11 Report, p. 31]]).

## What Happened
- **The protocols** assumed a hijacked plane would be readily identifiable, that there would be time to work through FAA and [[North American Aerospace Defense Command|NORAD]] chains of command, and that the hijacking would take the traditional form, not a suicide hijacking ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=35|9/11 Report, p. 18]]). They "did not contemplate an intercept" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=35|9/11 Report, p. 18]]).
- **The shootdown order:** Vice President Cheney authorized fighters to engage an inbound aircraft between about 10:10 and 10:15, saying he based this on an earlier conversation with the President; a 10:18 call obtained the President's confirmation ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=57|9/11 Report, pp. 40–41]]). By then United 93 had already crashed, at 10:03 ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=47|9/11 Report, p. 30]]).
- **Two sets of rules:** the NORAD fighters from Langley never received an engagement order, while fighters launched from Andrews at the Secret Service's request flew "weapons free" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=61|9/11 Report, p. 44]]); see [[Weapons Free]].

> [!caution] Sources disagree
> Cheney "based this authority on two calls made to President Bush, one shortly after 10:00, the other at 10:18" (Grammar, wk 2) vs. there is "no documentary evidence" for the earlier call, and the 10:18 call came after the authorization, to confirm it (9/11 Report, pp. 40–41).

## What Went Wrong
- The FAA did not join the Defense Department's air threat conference call for its first 48 minutes ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=55|9/11 Report, p. 38]]).
- NORAD did not know United 93 had been hijacked until after it crashed, so the military did not have 47 minutes to respond, as earlier accounts implied ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=51|9/11 Report, pp. 34, 44]]).

## Lessons
- NORAD officials maintain they would have intercepted United 93; the Commission: "We are not so sure" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=62|9/11 Report, p. 45]]).
- Its summary: NORAD and the FAA "were unprepared for the type of attacks launched against the United States on September 11, 2001" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=62|9/11 Report, p. 45]]). See [[September 11 Attacks (2001)]].
