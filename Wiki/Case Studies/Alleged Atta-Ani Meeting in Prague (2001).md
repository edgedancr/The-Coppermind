> [!summary]
> After [[September 11 Attacks (2001)|9/11]], a single source of the Czech intelligence service reported seeing hijacker Mohamed Atta meet an Iraqi diplomat, Ahmad al Ani, in Prague on April 9, 2001; the story was widely reported.[^1] The 9/11 Commission weighed the claim against other evidence and concluded that =="the available evidence does not support the original Czech report"==.[^2]

## What Happened
- The FBI's legal attaché and the Czech officers who met the source were "70 percent sure" he was sincere and believed his own story; the Czech service then said publicly there was a 70 percent probability the meeting took place.[^1]

## What Went Wrong
The evidence against the report:[^3]
- A bank camera put Atta in Virginia Beach on April 4, and he was in Coral Springs, Florida, on April 11; his phone was used to call Florida lodgings from Florida cell sites on April 6, 9, 10, and 11, though the Commission cannot confirm he placed the calls.
- No U.S. records show Atta leaving the country, and Czech flight, border, and photo records show no sign of him.
- The Czech government says Ani was about 70 miles from Prague until the afternoon of April 9, while the source was firm on 11:00 a.m.; Ani, in custody, denies any contact.
- Atta usually traveled under his true name, and no fraudulent passport for him has been found; [[Khalid Sheikh Mohammed|KSM]] and Ramzi Binalshibh both deny the meeting.

## Lessons
- The Commission did not claim certainty: its findings "cannot absolutely rule out the possibility" that Atta was in Prague.[^2]
- The case shows a source's sincerity being judged separately from the accuracy of his report. Compare [[Diagnostic Evidence]] and [[Analysis of Competing Hypotheses]].
