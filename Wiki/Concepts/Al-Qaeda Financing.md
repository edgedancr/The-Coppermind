> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission found that the [[September 11 Attacks (2001)|9/11]] plotters spent between $400,000 and $500,000, nearly all supplied by al-Qaeda through [[Khalid Sheikh Mohammed]], and that their financial tradecraft "was not especially sophisticated, but it was good enough" to defeat the detection mechanisms of the time.[^1] The CIA estimated al-Qaeda's pre-9/11 costs at about ==$30 million a year, raised almost entirely through donations==.[^2]

## Key Points
**Where the money came from**[^3]
- Not [[Usama Bin Ladin|Bin Ladin]]'s fortune: rather than a $300 million inheritance, he received about $1 million a year from roughly 1970 to 1994, and the Saudi government froze the proceeds when his share of the family company was sold.[^2]
- A core group of financial facilitators raised money from donors, mainly in the Gulf and particularly Saudi Arabia, relying on imams willing to divert zakat (charitable giving) and on employees of corrupt charities, or whole charities, siphoning funds.[^2]
- The Commission found no government other than the Taliban funding al-Qaeda before 9/11, and no evidence that the Saudi government as an institution or senior Saudi officials individually funded it, though charities with significant Saudi government sponsorship may have diverted funds.[^4]

**How it moved and spent it**[^4]
- Mostly by [[Hawala|hawala]], because Afghanistan's banking system was antiquated and formal banking was risky after the [[East Africa Embassy Bombings (1998)|1998 embassy bombings]].
- It spent funds as fast as it received them: on salaries, camps, weapons, and $10–20 million a year to the Taliban for safe haven; operations were a small part of the budget.

**Claims the Commission rejected:** it found no reliable evidence that al-Qaeda funded itself through drug trafficking or African conflict diamonds, and no evidence that anyone profited from advance knowledge of the attacks through securities trades.[^5]


**After 9/11**[^6]
- Early efforts to "starve the terrorists of money" by designating financiers and freezing assets appeared to have little effect; the Commission compared them to trying "to catch one kind of fish by draining the ocean".[^7]
- A better strategy targeted the relatively small number of financial facilitators at the core of al-Qaeda's revenue stream; their death or capture reduced its money and yielded intelligence.[^8] Public designations remained, but as "a form of diplomacy" rather than the primary weapon.[^8]
- The Commission recommended that tracking terrorist financing "remain front and center"; see [[Global Strategy Against Islamist Terrorism]].[^8]
## Why It Matters
- The origin of the 9/11 money remained unknown, but the Commission judged the question "of little practical significance": al-Qaeda had many avenues of funding.[^9]
- For financial intelligence, see [[Economic Intelligence]].
