> [!summary]
> Intro to Intelligence taught the U.S. classification levels by how much damage public release could cause, along with control systems and releasability markings that further limit who may see information.[^1] Access within those limits follows the principle of need to know.[^2]

## Key Points
**Levels**[^1]
1. **Unclassified**
2. **LES and FOUO:** law enforcement sensitive; for official use only (meets at least one Freedom of Information Act exemption criterion).
3. **Confidential:** release could cause *some* damage.
4. **Secret:** release could cause *serious* damage.
5. **Top Secret:** release could cause ==*exceptionally grave* damage==; people could die.

Lowenthal's definitions match: Confidential information might cause damage to national security if disclosed, Secret serious damage, and Top Secret exceptionally grave damage.[^3]

The midterm shorthand: serious damage means Secret, grave damage means Top Secret.[^2]

**Control systems**[^1]
- **SI:** protects the monitoring of foreign communications signals from satellites and other intelligence sources.
- **TK:** protects collection tasking, imagery and signals processing and exploitation techniques, and intelligence from overhead programs.
- **SI/TK:** both.

**Releasability**[^1]
- **Five Eyes:** USA, Australia, Canada, Great Britain, and New Zealand.
- **Three Eyes:** USA, Australia, and Great Britain.
- **USA NOFORN:** not releasable outside the U.S. community.

**Need to know:** giving only the information someone needs to do their job, and nothing more.[^2] The 9/11 Commission argued that a need-to-know culture should give way to "need to share," and that security rules nurture overclassification.[^4] See [[Need to Share]].

## Why It Matters
- Access to classified information runs through the clearance process; see [[Polygraph Examination]].
