> [!summary]
> Rob Johnston found that analysts, managers, and instructors used "tradecraft" as a catchall for the often-idiosyncratic methods of analysis, a word borrowed from the prestige of intelligence operations ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=37|Johnston, pp. 17–18]]). He argues the borrowing is a mistake: ==analysis "is neither craft nor art" but part of a scientific process== ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=37|Johnston, p. 17]]). What matters is that methods be explicit enough for others to test ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=39|Johnston, p. 19]]).

## Key Points
**What "tradecraft" implies** ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=38|Johnston, p. 18]])
- A mysterious process learned only by the initiated.
- Methods that are informal, idiosyncratic, unverifiable, and perhaps unexplainable.
- "Good" methods are simply those that survive, with no formal system for measuring their validity or reliability.

**Analysis as science**
- Analysts described anomaly detection, pattern recognition, scenarios, and weighing diagnostic evidence ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=38|Johnston, pp. 18–19]]). Johnston argues these can be formalized and replicated once their parameters, variables, and rules of evidence are made explicit ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=39|Johnston, p. 19]]).
- **The scientific method, broadly** ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=39|Johnston, p. 19]]): observe and describe phenomena; form hypotheses; have independent experts test them; refute or confirm them.
- Many analysts resisted the idea: "We're not scientists; we're analysts" ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=40|Johnston, p. 20]]).

**Costs of tradecraft**
- Quality depends on an individual's innate ability and the luck of finding a good mentor ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=40|Johnston, p. 20]]).
- Lessons passed on by apprenticeship are lost when experts retire ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=40|Johnston, p. 20]]).
- Ad hoc methods are seldom tested against competing methods, so analysts choose what they know or what deadlines allow ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=130|Johnston, p. 114]]).

**The medicine parallel:** medicine is also art, tradecraft, and science, but basic sciences revolutionized its practice; intelligence analysis "has not experienced that revolution," because the human sciences beneath it are harder to control ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=62|Johnston, p. 43]]).

**His conclusion:** developing the science is easy; the hard part is changing practitioners' perceptions and the culture of tradecraft ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=41|Johnston, p. 21]]). A research agenda that collects effectiveness data is the first step from a tradecraft model to a scientific one ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=130|Johnston, p. 114]]).

> [!question] Whose view is this?
> This is Johnston's argument. Elsewhere he describes analysis, "as it is now practiced," as "art, tradecraft, and science" ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=62|Johnston, p. 43]]), which is how Joseph Gartin quotes him ([[Gartin - The Future of Analysis (2019).pdf#page=3|Gartin, p. 3]]). Stephen Marrin surveys the wider debate over whether analysis can be scientific; see [[Analytic Objectivity]].
