> [!summary]
> Kaiser and Pherson treat outlining as part of thinking, not only writing: the more detailed the outline, the better the odds of insightful and accurate analysis.[^1] ==The goal is a roadmap down to the paragraph level==, whose main points become the paper's [[Topic Sentence|topic sentences]].[^2]

## Key Points
- **Start with a working title and a lead sentence,** or a working summary for a longer paper. A writer who cannot write the lead sentence needs to do more analysis.[^1] See [[Analytic Title]].
- **A precise [[Analytic Mission Statement|mission statement]]** makes the paper easier to organize, because a well-organized paper takes the shortest path to its judgments.[^1]
- **Fit the analysis to the facts,** not the facts to the analysis.[^1]
- **Why an outline stalls**:[^2]
	1. The writer has not focused on the audience's interests.
	2. The writer has not yet immersed themselves in the information.
	3. The writer has the facts but has not assembled them into a coherent story.
- **A filing system:** file each piece of information, not each report, by whether it concerns what happened, why, the impact, where it might go, or what it means for the audience; then look for patterns and tag each data point with a descriptor.[^2]
- **A shared wiki page** lets collaborating analysts pool data and interpretations as they work, which keeps individual mindsets from hardening.[^3]
- **Iterate:** new information and insights change the outline, and the cycle does not end until the paper is published.[^4]

## Why It Matters
- It turns the [[Analytic Line of March]] into paragraphs; Kaiser and Pherson's worked example outlines a paper on colonial Massachusetts for "the King of France".[^5]
