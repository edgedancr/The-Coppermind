> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission considered and rejected a new domestic intelligence agency, an "American MI-5," and recommended that the FBI keep counterterrorism intelligence collection inside the United States while building a specialized national security workforce ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=440|9/11 Report, pp. 423–426]]). Its concern was that ==the FBI "has long favored its criminal justice mission over its national security mission"== ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=440|9/11 Report, p. 423]]).

## Key Points
**Background** ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=91|9/11 Report, pp. 74–75]])
- The FBI's domestic intelligence work dates from the 1930s, when President Roosevelt had J. Edgar Hoover investigate foreign and foreign-inspired subversion.
- The [[Church and Pike Committees|Church and Pike investigations]] disclosed a covert program run from 1956 to 1971 against domestic organizations and dissidents, and unlawful wiretaps; Americans' "highly favorable" view of the FBI fell from 84 to 37 percent, and its Domestic Intelligence Division was dissolved.
- Attorney General guidelines (Levi in 1976, revised by Smith in 1983) then regulated domestic security investigations.

**Why not a new agency** ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=440|9/11 Report, pp. 423–424]])
- The analogy is weak: Britain's Security Service combines duties spread across several U.S. agencies ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=440|9/11 Report, p. 423]]).
- The FBI is used to collecting sensitive intelligence within the law; outside the Justice Department, legal oversight could become harder, and abuses of civil liberties could cause a backlash.
- A new agency would distract counterterrorism officials while the threat was high and add to information-sharing problems.
- It would have to build what the FBI already has: 28,000 employees, 56 field offices, informants, and ties to state, local, and foreign agencies.
- Counterterrorism cases quickly become criminal cases, and with [[The Wall (FBI)|"the wall"]] removed, one agency can use the full range of tools.

**What the Commission recommended** ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=442|9/11 Report, pp. 425–427]])
- A specialized, integrated national security workforce of agents, analysts, linguists, and surveillance specialists.
- Basic training for all new agents in both criminal justice and national security, then specialization; certification of all senior managers as intelligence officers.
- A deputy for national security in each field office, a budget aligned to four programs, and regular reports to Congress on field priorities and analysts.

**Reforms under way in 2004:** Director Robert Mueller had created an Office of Intelligence and field intelligence groups, but the Commission found gaps between announced reforms and the field, where managers could still put local concerns ahead of national security ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=442|9/11 Report, p. 425]]).

## Why It Matters
- The Commission urged a reciprocal relationship with state and local police through the [[Joint Terrorism Task Force|Joint Terrorism Task Forces]] ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=444|9/11 Report, p. 427]]).
- For the FBI's pre-9/11 priorities, see [[September 11 Attacks (2001)]].
