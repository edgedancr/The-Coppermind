> [!summary]
> Semiconductor export controls are U.S.-led restrictions on China's access to advanced AI chips and the equipment needed to make them. RAND researchers write that they have ==cut off China's access to advanced computing chips critical to AI== and delayed its efforts to mass-produce domestic AI chips by years ([[RAND - Full Stack, China's Evolving Industrial Policy for AI (2025).pdf|Chan et al., "U.S. Export Controls to Constrain China's Compute"]]). In an Intro to Intelligence guest lecture, CDR Blocker said U.S. controls on NVIDIA A100 and H100 GPUs are explicitly about limiting China's AI growth ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#10/29/26|Intro notes, 10/29/26]]).

## Key Points
**Effects** ([[RAND - Full Stack, China's Evolving Industrial Policy for AI (2025).pdf|Chan et al., "U.S. Export Controls to Constrain China's Compute"]])
- Controls on chipmaking equipment date back to 2018. Chinese firms such as ByteDance and Baidu already complain of being short on compute.
- The U.S. holds a large lead in total compute, partly because of the controls. Research cited by RAND puts China at about 15 percent of total AI compute and the U.S. at about 75 percent ([[RAND - Full Stack, China's Evolving Industrial Policy for AI (2025).pdf|Chan et al., "Computing Infrastructure"]]).
- Of 321 notable AI models with known hardware, 319 were trained on U.S. chips; even DeepSeek's recent training run used Nvidia GPUs ([[RAND - Full Stack, China's Evolving Industrial Policy for AI (2025).pdf|Chan et al., "Chips"]]).

**China's response** ([[RAND - Full Stack, China's Evolving Industrial Policy for AI (2025).pdf|Chan et al., "U.S. Export Controls to Constrain China's Compute"]])
- At an April 2025 Politburo meeting, Xi Jinping called for "self-reliance" and an "autonomously controllable" AI hardware and software ecosystem.
- Substitutes: Huawei's Ascend chips, alternatives to Nvidia's CUDA software, and the MindSpore and PaddlePaddle frameworks, all of which still lag.
- Workarounds: stockpiling chips, smuggling, and building data centers abroad, from Mexico to Malaysia.
- RAND's assessment: the controls are unlikely to halt China's AI progress and will likely bolster parts of its chip industry.

**As a strategy** ([[RAND - Artificial General Intelligence's Five Hard National Security Problems (2025).pdf|Mitre & Predd, "Toward a Robust Strategy"]])
- Mitre and Predd write that a control regime embodying the "small yard, high fence" policy appears to have produced a five-year gap in advanced semiconductors.
- They warn that leaning on compute for advantage could be brittle if the controls aren't enforced, if China's chip industry catches up, or if AGI proves reachable with less compute.

**How far behind is China?** The sources measure differently:
- CDR Blocker: still 3–5 generations behind in cutting-edge logic manufacturing ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#10/29/26|Intro notes, 10/29/26]]).
- Mitre and Predd: a five-year gap in advanced semiconductors ([[RAND - Artificial General Intelligence's Five Hard National Security Problems (2025).pdf|Mitre & Predd, "Toward a Robust Strategy"]]).
- Chan et al.: chipmaking capabilities remain years behind leaders such as TSMC ([[RAND - Full Stack, China's Evolving Industrial Policy for AI (2025).pdf|Chan et al., "Chips"]]).

## Why It Matters
- CDR Blocker framed the stakes: controlling AI chips means controlling the pace of AI development ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#10/29/26|Intro notes, 10/29/26]]). For the chip industry itself, see [[Semiconductors and National Security]]; for China's broader policy, see [[China's AI Industrial Policy]].
