> [!summary]
> Intro to Intelligence taught that intelligence writing should be **clear**, **concise**, **informative**, **timely**, and **tailored** ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/15/26|Intro notes, 9/15/26]]). Its goal is not to please the reader's literary sensibilities, but to give the facts and then either recommend a course of action or explain the implications ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/15/26|Intro notes, 9/15/26]]).

## Key Points
All from ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/15/26|Intro notes, 9/15/26]]) unless noted.
- **Lead with the point.** ==The first sentence states the recommendations or implications.== The writer isn't telling a story to keep the reader engaged, but getting information to them as fast as possible. The reader should immediately understand the piece's purpose and perspective.
- **Paragraphs:** the first sentence summarizes the paragraph. A topic sentence assumes the reader doesn't yet have the paragraph's information; a concluding sentence assumes they do, so it can say something different. See [[Topic Sentence]].
- **Sentences:**
	- Make each sentence count: no fluff, no unneeded description.
	- Use the active voice.
	- Avoid dangling prepositions and long dependent clauses ("log" in my notes).
	- Skip qualifiers like "very" or "horrible." Give specifics, not opinions.
- **Editing:** be your own ruthless editor. Leave the draft for at least 30 minutes, then reread it; you will find improvements.
- **Key judgments:** the 30-second "so what" summary of a report ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#10/13/26|Intro notes, 10/13/26]]). See [[Key Judgments]].

## Why It Matters
- Most readers read only the topic sentences and summary ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/3/26|Intro notes, 9/3/26]]), so the opening sentences carry the product.
- For defining a term clearly, see the [[SEE-It Framework]] ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/1/26|Intro notes, 9/1/26]]).
