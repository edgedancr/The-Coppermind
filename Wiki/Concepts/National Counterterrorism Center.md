> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission recommended a National Counterterrorism Center (NCTC), built on the existing [[Terrorist Threat Integration Center]], as "a center for joint operational planning and joint intelligence, staffed by personnel from the various agencies" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=420|9/11 Report, p. 403]]). It modeled the idea on the military's unified joint commands: the NCTC would combine intelligence (the Joint Staff's J-2) and operational planning (J-3), while overall policy stayed with the [[National Security Council]] ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=420|9/11 Report, p. 403]]).

## Key Points
**Why** ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=417|9/11 Report, pp. 400–403]])
- In each missed opportunity before 9/11, "no one was firmly in charge of managing the case" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=417|9/11 Report, p. 400]]). The Commission found labels like "[[Metaphors for Intelligence|connecting the dots]]" too narrow: they "describe the symptoms, not the disease" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=417|9/11 Report, p. 400]]).
- ==Cooperation is not joint action==: when agencies cooperate, one defines the problem and seeks help; when they act jointly, the problem and options are defined together from the start ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=417|9/11 Report, p. 400]]). The Commission asked witnesses, "Who is the quarterback?" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=417|9/11 Report, p. 400]])
- Terrorism analysis was duplicated across the TTIC, the CIA's [[Counterterrorist Center (CIA)|Counterterrorist Center]], DIA, and Homeland Security, with too few experts to go around ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=418|9/11 Report, p. 401]]).
- Donald Rumsfeld told the Commission the executive branch was "stove-piped much like the four services were nearly 20 years ago," before the [[Goldwater-Nichols Act]] ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=420|9/11 Report, p. 403]]). See [[Stovepipe]].

**What it would do** ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=421|9/11 Report, pp. 404–405]])
- **Intelligence:** lead strategic analysis, pooling all-source intelligence, foreign and domestic; produce net assessments; provide warning; task collection inside and outside the U.S.
- **Operations:** plan jointly and assign lead agencies, but leave execution to them, then track implementation.
- **Not policymaking:** it would follow the President's and the NSC's direction.
- **Authorities:** a head appointed by the President, confirmed by the Senate, reporting to a [[Director of National Intelligence|National Intelligence Director]], with a say in choosing the leaders of agencies' counterterrorism units.

**The Commission's example:** if NSA found a suspected terrorist traveling to Bangkok and Kuala Lumpur, the NCTC would analyze the travelers' identities, assign tasks to the CIA overseas, Homeland Security at U.S. entry points, and the FBI, and track the case ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=421|9/11 Report, pp. 404–405]]).

## Why It Matters
- The Commission named its "most serious disadvantage" as "the reverse of its greatest virtue": concentrating too much power in one place ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=423|9/11 Report, p. 406]]).
- It closed the section with Thomas Schelling's comment on Pearl Harbor: surprise "is likely to be a complicated, diffuse, bureaucratic thing" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=423|9/11 Report, p. 406]]).
