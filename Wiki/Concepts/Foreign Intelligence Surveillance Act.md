> [!summary]
> The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 regulates intelligence collection aimed at foreign powers and agents of foreign powers inside the United States, and requires court review of proposed surveillance (later also physical searches).[^1] The 9/11 Commission traces "the wall" between intelligence and criminal work to how FISA was interpreted and applied.[^2]

## Key Points
**How the rules developed**[^3]
- Before 1978 the attorney general could authorize surveillance of foreign powers and their agents without court review.
- Courts read the act to allow a search only if its **"primary purpose"** was to obtain foreign intelligence, so FISA could not be used to get around criminal warrant requirements. The Justice Department took this to mean prosecutors could be briefed on FISA information but could not direct or control its collection.
- After the 1994 Aldrich Ames prosecution, the Justice Department's Office of Intelligence Policy and Review (OIPR), which presents FISA applications to the court, began imposing its own sharing procedures and became the gatekeeper for FISA information.
- Attorney General Reno's July 1995 procedures required sharing intelligence with prosecutors but regulated how; they were "almost immediately misunderstood and misapplied".[^4]

**How the barriers grew**[^5]
- OIPR became the sole gatekeeper to the Criminal Division, and agents came to believe, wrongly, that no FISA information, and later no intelligence at all, could go to criminal investigators.
- Grand jury secrecy, executive-order limits on mixing domestic and foreign intelligence, and NSA caveats requiring approval before sharing "further blocked the arteries of information sharing".[^6]

**The probable-cause problem in 2001:** to search Zacarias Moussaoui's laptop under FISA, the FBI had to show he was an agent of a foreign power, which a criminal warrant does not require; FBI headquarters judged his link to a Chechen rebel leader insufficient and did not submit an application.[^7] See [[Late Leads Before September 11 (2001)]].

## Why It Matters
- The Commission concluded that in August 2001 "everyone involved was confused about the rules governing the sharing and use of information gathered in intelligence channels".[^8] See [[The Wall (FBI)]] and the [[Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court]].
