> [!summary]
> AI succession is [[Richard Sutton]]'s term for the future in which digital intelligence, or humans augmented by it, comes to hold the resources and power humans now hold. Sutton calls ==succession "inevitable,"== though it could turn out well or badly, and he encourages people to think positively about it.[^1]

## Key Points
**Sutton's four-part argument**[^1]
1. No government or organization gives humanity a unified, dominant point of view on how the world should be run.
2. Researchers will eventually figure out how intelligence works.
3. We won't stop at human-level intelligence; we will reach superintelligence.
4. Over time, the most intelligent things around will inevitably gain resources and power.

**Why he views it positively**[^1]
- Understanding intelligence is something humans have tried to do for thousands of years, and he calls it "a great success for science, humanities."
- From "the point of view of the universe," he sees a transition from replicators (humans, animals, plants, which can be copied but not understood) to designed entities that can themselves design. He calls it one of four great stages: dust gives rise to stars, stars to planets, planets to life, and now life to designed entities.
- Whether we treat AIs as our offspring or as "not us" is, he says, "our choice."
- For most people, he notes, little influence over the future is lost: most of humanity already has no say over who controls atomic bombs or nation states. He calls the current situation "pretty bad," and so is open to change.

**Dwarkesh Patel's pushback**[^1]
- Future generations of humans could have values we would be right to fear; Patel's example is "Nazis were humans." Kinship doesn't answer concerns about entities we don't fully understand gaining power this fast.
- Some change is good and some is not: the Industrial Revolution versus the Bolshevik Revolution.
- As with raising children, we can try to give AIs "robust and steerable and prosocial values," or "high integrity": refusing harmful requests and being honest.

**Sutton's reply**[^1]
- He asks whether there are universal values everyone can agree on.
- He urges avoiding "the feeling of entitlement," focusing on our own local goals and families, and seeking change that is voluntary rather than imposed.

## Why It Matters
- Compare the [[Existential Risk]] page: SAIL describes the danger as powerful systems pursuing goals without sufficient human control,[^2] while Sutton asks how much control a particular species on a particular planet should have over the future.[^1]
