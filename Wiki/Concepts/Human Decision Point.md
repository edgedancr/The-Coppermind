> [!summary]
> SAIL's rule for where humans belong once AI enters a pipeline: ==AI belongs in the speed, volume, scale, and language work; humans belong in judgment, accountability, and policy==.[^1]

## Key Points
**In the intelligence pipeline**[^1]
- *AI:* collection, processing, and part of analysis.
- *Humans:* part of analysis, deciding, and acting.

**In the kill chain**[^1]
- *AI:* classification, tracking, and collateral estimation (find, fix, track, target), and [[Battle Damage Assessment|battle damage assessment]] algorithms (assess).
- *Human:* engage, because it is a legal, moral, and policy requirement.
- **The LOAC mandate:** the [[Law of Armed Conflict]] requires distinction and proportionality, and neither can be delegated to an algorithm.

**In police intelligence:** in an Intro to Intelligence guest lecture, Detective Mennen said that ==AI and link analysis speed the first cut, but vetting, law, and ethics still belong to people==.[^2] See [[Intelligence-Led Policing]].

**In the ODNI AI Ethics Framework:** ODNI says ==the assessed risk determines at which point, and to what degree, a human is involved== in an AI, since IC uses range from basic business tasks to highly sensitive analysis.[^3] Where an AI could have significant consequences, such as depriving people of constitutional rights, ODNI asks how individual human involvement and accountability will be ensured, and whether the human should step in before results are used in analysis or before outputs go to follow-on uses.[^3] It asks who the accountable human is, whether they know they hold that role, and what they need to know about the AI to judge its reliability and accuracy.[^3] See [[ODNI AI Ethics Framework]].

## Why It Matters
- The [[Time Compression Trap]] and [[Automation Bias]] both threaten the human decision point.[^1]
- ODNI cautions that adding an accountable human can itself introduce cognitive biases, including [[Confirmation Bias|confirmation bias]].[^3]
