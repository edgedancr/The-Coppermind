> [!summary]
> Analytic culture is the set of norms, rewards, and habits that shape how intelligence analysts actually work. Rob Johnston, an anthropologist who began fieldwork in the Intelligence Community four days after 9/11, built his 2005 study on 489 interviews, focus groups, and observation ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=13|Johnston, p. xiii]]). He concluded that ==one cannot separate the cognitive aspects of intelligence analysis from its cultural context== ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=26|Johnston, p. 6]]). Gregory Treverton's foreword sums up the findings: no baseline analytic method, a bias toward confirming earlier views, and a process driven by current intelligence ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=11|Treverton, foreword, p. xi]]).

## Key Points
**Johnston's findings** ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=29|Johnston, pp. 9–29]])
- **[[Secrecy vs. Efficacy]]:** secrecy limits access to the information accurate analysis needs; he perceived more emphasis on secrecy than on effectiveness ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=31|Johnston, p. 11]]).
- **Time constraints:** most analysts named time as one of their greatest constraints and described a communitywide shift toward short-term products ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=33|Johnston, p. 13]]). One recalled having 60 percent of their time for long-term products 15 years earlier, and 20 to 25 percent now ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=33|Johnston, p. 13]]). See [[Current vs. Long-Term Intelligence]].
- **Focus on current production:** analysts said they were too busy writing to test hypotheses, and that formal techniques took too long ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=35|Johnston, p. 15]]).
- **Rewards and incentives:** analysts saw promotion as tied to the number of daily products and to recognition by senior policymakers ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=36|Johnston, p. 16]]); see [[President's Daily Brief]]. One summed it up: "sheer production equals promotion" ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=36|Johnston, p. 16]]).
- **Tradecraft vs. science:** see [[Tradecraft vs. Science in Intelligence Analysis]] ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=37|Johnston, pp. 17–21]]).
- **[[Confirmation Bias]], norms, and taboos:** the bias he identified most often, driven mainly by the need to maintain a [[Corporate Judgment]] ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=41|Johnston, pp. 21–23]]), and by weighing secret sources more heavily than open ones ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=44|Johnston, p. 24]]).
- **Analytic identity:** analysts described their work process or their office ("I work counterterrorism") rather than a discipline of their own ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=46|Johnston, p. 26]]), and many compared the job to reporting ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=47|Johnston, p. 27]]).
- **Training:** agencies trained analysts separately, if at all, so analysts struggled to find and work with each other across agencies ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=48|Johnston, pp. 28–29]]).

> [!question] Whose view is this?
> The findings are what analysts told Johnston and what he observed after 9/11, when, he writes, many were angry about the attack and felt guilty that it happened on their watch. He notes the study has no baseline to test its data against ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=13|Johnston, p. xiii]]). He also warns that he grew to empathize with analysts, and asks readers to judge the findings critically ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=30|Johnston, p. 10]]). His fieldwork predates the 2015 version of [[ICD 203 Analytic Standards|ICD 203]].

## Why It Matters
- Johnston's recommendations respond to these findings; see [[Johnston's Analytic Culture Recommendations]] and [[Performance Improvement Infrastructure]].
- A retired CIA operations officer, Joseph Hayes, wrote in the afterword that Johnston described "a world that I find all too familiar" ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=172|Hayes, afterword, p. 160]]).
- [inferred] The time and rewards findings help explain the 9/11 Commission's description of a CIA analytic culture that moved toward "the culture of the newsroom" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=108|9/11 Report, p. 91]]).
