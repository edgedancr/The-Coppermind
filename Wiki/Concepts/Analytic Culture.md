> [!summary]
> Analytic culture is the set of norms, rewards, and habits that shape how intelligence analysts actually work. [[Rob Johnston]], an anthropologist who began fieldwork in the [[United States Intelligence Community|Intelligence Community]] four days after [[September 11 Attacks (2001)|9/11]], built his 2005 study on 489 interviews, focus groups, and observation.[^1] He concluded that ==one cannot separate the cognitive aspects of [[Intelligence Analysis|intelligence analysis]] from its cultural context==.[^2] Gregory Treverton's foreword sums up the findings: no baseline analytic method, a bias toward confirming earlier views, and a process driven by current intelligence.[^3]

## Key Points
**Johnston's findings**[^4]
- **[[Secrecy vs. Efficacy]]:** secrecy limits access to the information accurate analysis needs; he perceived more emphasis on secrecy than on effectiveness.[^5]
- **Time constraints:** most analysts named time as one of their greatest constraints and described a communitywide shift toward short-term products.[^6] One recalled having 60 percent of their time for long-term products 15 years earlier, and 20 to 25 percent now.[^6] See [[Current vs. Long-Term Intelligence]].
- **Focus on current production:** analysts said they were too busy writing to test hypotheses, and that formal techniques took too long.[^7]
- **Rewards and incentives:** analysts saw promotion as tied to the number of daily products and to recognition by senior policymakers;[^8] see [[President's Daily Brief]]. One summed it up: "sheer production equals promotion".[^8]
- **Tradecraft vs. science:** see [[Tradecraft vs. Science in Intelligence Analysis]].[^9]
- **[[Confirmation Bias]], norms, and taboos:** the bias he identified most often, driven mainly by the need to maintain a [[Corporate Judgment]],[^10] and by weighing secret sources more heavily than open ones.[^11]
- **Analytic identity:** analysts described their work process or their office ("I work counterterrorism") rather than a discipline of their own,[^12] and many compared the job to reporting.[^13]
- **Training:** agencies trained analysts separately, if at all, so analysts struggled to find and work with each other across agencies.[^14]

> [!question] Whose view is this?
> The findings are what analysts told Johnston and what he observed after 9/11, when, he writes, many were angry about the attack and felt guilty that it happened on their watch. He notes the study has no baseline to test its data against.[^1] He also warns that he grew to empathize with analysts, and asks readers to judge the findings critically.[^15] His fieldwork predates the 2015 version of [[ICD 203 Analytic Standards|ICD 203]].

## Why It Matters
- Johnston's recommendations respond to these findings; see [[Johnston's Analytic Culture Recommendations]] and [[Performance Improvement Infrastructure]].
- A retired CIA operations officer, Joseph Hayes, wrote in the afterword that Johnston described "a world that I find all too familiar".[^16]
- [inferred] The time and rewards findings help explain the 9/11 Commission's description of a CIA analytic culture that moved toward "the culture of the newsroom".[^17]
