> [!summary]
> Intelligence officers often describe their role as "telling truth to power," and the [[Director of National Intelligence|DNI]]'s 2017 ethics principles say "speak truth to power".[^1] Mark Lowenthal calls the idea ==wrong, dangerous, and arrogant==, because intelligence conveys uncertainty rather than truth.[^2]

## Key Points
**Lowenthal's case**
- **Origin:** the phrase is credited to the civil rights leader Bayard Rustin, a Quaker, in 1942; a 1955 Quaker pamphlet on nonviolence took it as its title.[^3]
- **Uncertainty:** analysts rarely have a complete picture. If analysis overstates certainty, policymakers are more likely to make bad decisions, and the fault lies with the analyst.[^4] "Truth is an absolute," so truth leaves no room for uncertainty.[^4]
- **Compromise:** most analysis comes from several analysts or agencies, and truth cannot be the outcome of a compromise.[^5]
- **Misplaced reliance:** claiming truth invites policymakers to rely on analysis more than it warrants. They may reject it for good reasons, including information analysts do not have.[^5]
- **A better phrase:** John Moseman's "speaking honesty to power," which admits fallibility.[^6]

**Marrin's case**
- The slogan embodies the "heroic, idealized myth" of the objective analyst.[^7]
- Jack Davis proposed a more realistic standard, "tell it like they THINK it is," which Marrin equates with "calling it as you see it".[^7]
- Jennifer Sims warns that analysts who claim the truth may forget that power and truth exist on both sides of the analyst-customer divide.[^7]

> [!question] Whose view is this?
> Lowenthal's article is a short perspective piece. The DNI's ethics principles still use the phrase.[^4]

## Why It Matters
- It matches the class point that intelligence is [[Intelligence (National Security)|"proximate reality," not truth]], and the reasoning behind [[Estimative Language]] and [[Analytic Objectivity]].
