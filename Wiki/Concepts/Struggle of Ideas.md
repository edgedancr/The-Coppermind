> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission argued that the United States "has to help defeat an ideology, not just a group of people".[^1] The small share of Muslims fully committed to Bin Ladin's version of Islam "are impervious to persuasion," so the effort aims at the large majority; but ==the United States "can promote moderation, but cannot ensure its ascendancy. Only Muslims can do this"==.[^2]

## Key Points
- **The question:** in 2003 Defense Secretary Rumsfeld asked whether the United States was capturing, killing, or deterring more terrorists every day "than the madrassas and the radical clerics are recruiting"; the Commission called these "the right questions".[^3]
- **The setting:** polls in 2002 found that only 15 percent of Egyptians and 12 percent of Saudis held a favorable opinion of the United States.[^4] The Commission described "a clash within a civilization," arising from conditions in the Muslim world.[^5]
- **Define the message:** offer moral leadership, "committed to treat people humanely, abide by the rule of law, and be generous",[^1] and stand for a better future where Muslim governments, even friends, do not respect these principles.[^1]
- **Policy is part of the message:** U.S. policy on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict and U.S. actions in Iraq dominate popular commentary across the Arab and Muslim world.[^1]
- **Defend ideals abroad,** as in the Cold War: expand broadcasting to the Arab world, Iran, and Afghanistan, and rebuild scholarship, exchange, and library programs.[^6]
- **An agenda of opportunity:** an International Youth Opportunity Fund for schools in Muslim states that invest in public education, and economic policies that encourage development and open societies.[^7] "Terrorism is not caused by poverty," but when societies break down, breeding grounds for terrorism are created.[^8]

## Why It Matters
- It is the second leg of the [[Global Strategy Against Islamist Terrorism]]. For the ideology itself, see [[Usama Bin Ladin]] and [[World Islamic Front Fatwa]].
