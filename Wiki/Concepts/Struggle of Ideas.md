> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission argued that the United States "has to help defeat an ideology, not just a group of people" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=393|9/11 Report, p. 376]]). The small share of Muslims fully committed to Bin Ladin's version of Islam "are impervious to persuasion," so the effort aims at the large majority; but ==the United States "can promote moderation, but cannot ensure its ascendancy. Only Muslims can do this"== ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=392|9/11 Report, pp. 375–376]]).

## Key Points
- **The question:** in 2003 Defense Secretary Rumsfeld asked whether the United States was capturing, killing, or deterring more terrorists every day "than the madrassas and the radical clerics are recruiting"; the Commission called these "the right questions" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=391|9/11 Report, pp. 374–375]]).
- **The setting:** polls in 2002 found that only 15 percent of Egyptians and 12 percent of Saudis held a favorable opinion of the United States ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=392|9/11 Report, p. 375]]). The Commission described "a clash within a civilization," arising from conditions in the Muslim world ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=380|9/11 Report, p. 363]]).
- **Define the message:** offer moral leadership, "committed to treat people humanely, abide by the rule of law, and be generous" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=393|9/11 Report, p. 376]]), and stand for a better future where Muslim governments, even friends, do not respect these principles ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=393|9/11 Report, p. 376]]).
- **Policy is part of the message:** U.S. policy on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict and U.S. actions in Iraq dominate popular commentary across the Arab and Muslim world ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=393|9/11 Report, p. 376]]).
- **Defend ideals abroad,** as in the Cold War: expand broadcasting to the Arab world, Iran, and Afghanistan, and rebuild scholarship, exchange, and library programs ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=394|9/11 Report, p. 377]]).
- **An agenda of opportunity:** an International Youth Opportunity Fund for schools in Muslim states that invest in public education, and economic policies that encourage development and open societies ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=395|9/11 Report, pp. 378–379]]). "Terrorism is not caused by poverty," but when societies break down, breeding grounds for terrorism are created ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=395|9/11 Report, p. 378]]).

## Why It Matters
- It is the second leg of the [[Global Strategy Against Islamist Terrorism]]. For the ideology itself, see [[Usama Bin Ladin]] and [[World Islamic Front Fatwa]].
