> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission judged congressional oversight of intelligence and counterterrorism "dysfunctional," and called strengthening it perhaps "among the most difficult and important" of its recommendations.[^1] It proposed either a joint committee on the model of the Joint Committee on Atomic Energy, or a single committee in each house with combined authorizing and appropriating powers.[^2]

## Key Points
**The problem**
- The House and Senate intelligence committees authorize intelligence funding, but the final budget review happens in the Defense subcommittees of the Appropriations committees, which have no intelligence subcommittees, and only a few members and staff review the requests.[^3]
- Under existing rules, the intelligence committees "lack the power, influence, and sustained capability" to meet the challenge.[^2]

**Before [[September 11 Attacks (2001)|9/11]]**[^4]
- The select committees, created in 1977, had limited authority: appropriations went through the Appropriations committees, and Armed Services held jurisdiction over the Defense intelligence agencies and, in the Senate, the CIA.[^5]
- Term limits kept members from building expertise, and secrecy denied them "democracy's best oversight mechanism: public disclosure".[^5] See [[Church and Pike Committees]].
- Terrorism fell under at least 14 committees in the House alone[^6] and was "a second- or third-order priority".[^7]

**Recommended features**[^8]
- Continuing studies of the agencies, reported to all members.
- A public top-line intelligence budget, with the [[National Intelligence Program]] appropriated to the National Intelligence Director rather than the secretary of defense.
- A subcommittee dedicated to oversight, freed from budget work.
- Subpoena authority, and a majority that never exceeds the minority by more than one.
- Four members who also sit on Armed Services, Judiciary, Foreign Affairs, and Defense Appropriations.
- Indefinite terms, so members build expertise, and small committees of perhaps seven or nine.
- A nonpartisan staff working for the whole committee.[^2]

**Homeland security:** Homeland Security's leaders appeared before 88 committees and subcommittees; the Commission recommended one principal point of oversight in each house.[^9]

## Why It Matters
- ==The Commission's other reforms "will not work if congressional oversight does not change too"==.[^2]
- For the committees as they stand, see [[Intelligence Oversight Committees]] and [[Gang of Eight]]. For the executive-branch reforms, see [[Director of National Intelligence]] and [[National Counterterrorism Center]].
