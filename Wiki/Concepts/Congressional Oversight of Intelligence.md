> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission judged congressional oversight of intelligence and counterterrorism "dysfunctional," and called strengthening it perhaps "among the most difficult and important" of its recommendations ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=436|9/11 Report, pp. 419–420]]). It proposed either a joint committee on the model of the Joint Committee on Atomic Energy, or a single committee in each house with combined authorizing and appropriating powers ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=437|9/11 Report, p. 420]]).

## Key Points
**The problem**
- The House and Senate intelligence committees authorize intelligence funding, but the final budget review happens in the Defense subcommittees of the Appropriations committees, which have no intelligence subcommittees, and only a few members and staff review the requests ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=427|9/11 Report, p. 410]]).
- Under existing rules, the intelligence committees "lack the power, influence, and sustained capability" to meet the challenge ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=437|9/11 Report, p. 420]]).

**Before 9/11** ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=120|9/11 Report, pp. 103–107]])
- The select committees, created in 1977, had limited authority: appropriations went through the Appropriations committees, and Armed Services held jurisdiction over the Defense intelligence agencies and, in the Senate, the CIA ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=120|9/11 Report, p. 103]]).
- Term limits kept members from building expertise, and secrecy denied them "democracy's best oversight mechanism: public disclosure" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=120|9/11 Report, p. 103]]). See [[Church and Pike Committees]].
- Terrorism fell under at least 14 committees in the House alone ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=122|9/11 Report, p. 105]]) and was "a second- or third-order priority" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=124|9/11 Report, p. 107]]).

**Recommended features** ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=437|9/11 Report, pp. 420–421]])
- Continuing studies of the agencies, reported to all members.
- A public top-line intelligence budget, with the [[National Intelligence Program]] appropriated to the National Intelligence Director rather than the secretary of defense.
- A subcommittee dedicated to oversight, freed from budget work.
- Subpoena authority, and a majority that never exceeds the minority by more than one.
- Four members who also sit on Armed Services, Judiciary, Foreign Affairs, and Defense Appropriations.
- Indefinite terms, so members build expertise, and small committees of perhaps seven or nine.
- A nonpartisan staff working for the whole committee ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=437|9/11 Report, p. 420]]).

**Homeland security:** Homeland Security's leaders appeared before 88 committees and subcommittees; the Commission recommended one principal point of oversight in each house ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=438|9/11 Report, p. 421]]).

## Why It Matters
- ==The Commission's other reforms "will not work if congressional oversight does not change too"== ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=437|9/11 Report, p. 420]]).
- For the committees as they stand, see [[Intelligence Oversight Committees]] and [[Gang of Eight]]. For the executive-branch reforms, see [[Director of National Intelligence]] and [[National Counterterrorism Center]].
