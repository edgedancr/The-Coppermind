> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission found that the pre-9/11 FBI was superb at investigating attacks after the fact but poorly built to prevent them: its 56 field offices set their own priorities, performance was measured by arrests and convictions, and it "lacked the ability to know what it knew".[^1] Its 1998 strategic plan made counterterrorism the top priority, but "the plan did not succeed".[^2]

## Key Points
**Structure and incentives**[^3]
- Each field office's special agent in charge was largely free to set priorities, which centered on traditional crimes such as white-collar offenses, drugs, and gangs: ==individual field offices served "local priorities, not national priorities."==
- Counterterrorism and counterintelligence cases, which might never produce quantifiable results, were not career-enhancing.
- Under the [[Office of Origin|office of origin]] system, the New York Field Office held all Bin Ladin cases, and other offices had little reason to invest in them.

**Why the 1998 plan failed**[^4]
- Resources did not shift: counterterrorism spending stayed flat from 1998 to 2001, and in 2000 twice as many agents worked drugs as counterterrorism.
- Analysis was weak: analysts were used tactically to support cases, and the FBI had never completed an assessment of the overall terrorist threat to the U.S. homeland.
- Collection was weak: only three days of the 16-week agents' course covered counterintelligence and counterterrorism, and there was a backlog of untranslated intercepts.
- Information systems were "woefully inadequate," with no reports officers to turn interviews into retrievable intelligence.

**MAXCAP 05:** Dale Watson's 2000 plan to bring the FBI to "maximum feasible capacity" in counterterrorism by 2005; by September 2001 almost every field office was judged to be below it.[^5] In the summer of 2001 Watson wished he had had "500 analysts looking at Usama Bin Ladin threat information instead of two".[^6]

## Why It Matters
- The Commission's summary: before 9/11, law enforcement could act only after specific individuals were identified or an attack had happened; the FBI and Justice Department "declare war by indicting someone".[^7]
- For the sharing barriers, see [[The Wall (FBI)]] and [[Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act]]; for the Commission's reforms, see [[Domestic Intelligence and the FBI]].
