> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission called "the human or systemic resistance to sharing information" the biggest impediment to all-source analysis ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=433|9/11 Report, p. 416]]). It urged agencies to replace a "need-to-know" culture of information protection with a ==**"need-to-share" culture of integration**== ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=434|9/11 Report, p. 417]]).

## Key Points
**What was wrong with need to know** ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=434|9/11 Report, p. 417]])
- It assumes one can know in advance who will need the information, and that the risk of disclosure outweighs the benefit of sharing; "those Cold War assumptions are no longer appropriate."
- Agencies felt they owned what they collected; the Commission wanted them to feel "a duty to the information."
- Security rules nurtured overclassification and excessive compartmentation; sharing carried risks but few rewards, and not sharing carried no punishment.
- In the 9/11 story, information was available but no one asked, was distributed only in a compartmented channel, or was asked for but could not be shared.

**What the Commission recommended** ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=434|9/11 Report, pp. 417–419]])
- Give incentives for sharing, and hold intelligence to the same standards "whether it is collected in Pakistan or in Texas" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=434|9/11 Report, p. 417]]).
- Separate data from sources and methods when a report is created, so it starts in its most shareable form, with further detail by query and an audit trail ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=434|9/11 Report, pp. 417–418]]). This reverses the [[Tearline|tearline]] practice ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=584|9/11 Report, p. 567, ch. 13 n. 16]]).
- Share horizontally: replace each agency's hub-and-spoke database with a decentralized network searchable across agency lines, protected by controlling access to data rather than to the whole network; the Commission praised the Markle Foundation's "trusted information network" framework ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=435|9/11 Report, p. 418]]).
- The President should lead, because "the policy and legal issues are harder than the technical ones" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=435|9/11 Report, pp. 418–419]]).

## Why It Matters
- The Commission did not dismiss security: counterintelligence concerns "are still real" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=434|9/11 Report, p. 417]]).
- Without a government-wide effort, agencies "may only be able to modernize the stovepipes, not replace them" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=435|9/11 Report, p. 418]]). See [[Stovepipe]], [[Originator Controlled]], and need to know in [[Security Classification]].
- The Commission's global strategy adds that guidelines for sharing, among agencies and with the private sector, should safeguard the privacy of the people the information is about ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=411|9/11 Report, p. 394]]).
