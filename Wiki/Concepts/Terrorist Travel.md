> [!summary]
> "For terrorists, travel documents are as important as weapons": to meet, train, and reach targets, they must surface to pass through regulated channels ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=401|9/11 Report, p. 384]]). The 9/11 Commission found that ==as many as 15 of the 19 hijackers were potentially vulnerable to interception by border authorities==, and recommended treating travel as "at least as powerful a weapon against terrorists as targeting their money" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=401|9/11 Report, pp. 384–385]]).

## Key Points
- **Before 9/11:** border security was not seen as a national security matter, and no agency systematically analyzed terrorists' travel strategies ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=401|9/11 Report, p. 384]]).
- **Evasive methods:** altered and counterfeit passports and visas, specific routes, corrupt officials, human smuggling networks, supportive travel agencies, and immigration and identity fraud, which "can sometimes be detected" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=401|9/11 Report, p. 384]]).
- **What could have worked:** analyzing travel documents and patterns could have let authorities intercept 4 to 15 hijackers, and better use of government databases could have identified up to 3 ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=401|9/11 Report, p. 384]]). Five conspirators were refused visas and one was denied entry ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=401|9/11 Report, p. 384]]).
- **Recommendations:**
	- Combine travel intelligence, operations, and law enforcement to intercept terrorists, find their travel facilitators, and constrain their mobility ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=402|9/11 Report, p. 385]]).
	- Fold border security into a wider network of screening points, including transportation and vital facilities ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=404|9/11 Report, p. 387]]).
	- Complete a biometric entry-exit screening system ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=406|9/11 Report, p. 389]]).
	- Exchange terrorist information with trusted allies and raise global border standards ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=407|9/11 Report, p. 390]]).
	- Set federal standards for birth certificates and sources of identification such as driver's licenses ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=407|9/11 Report, p. 390]]).

## Why It Matters
- It is part of the third leg of the [[Global Strategy Against Islamist Terrorism]]. For pre-9/11 screening, see [[TIPOFF Watchlist]], [[No-Fly List]], and [[Layered Aviation Security]]; for the hijackers' forged documents, see [[Planes Operation]].
