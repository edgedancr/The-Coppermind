> [!summary]
> Bottom line up front (BLUF) means leading with the main point. In the BLUF format, the first sentence of each paragraph sums up everything in it, and the details follow from most to least important ([[BLUF Writing Format - How to Write an Intelligence Product (Henley-Putnam).pdf#page=2|BLUF handout, p. 2]]). ==Policymakers and commanders are often too busy to read every word, so leading with the point lets them skim without missing what matters== ([[BLUF Writing Format - How to Write an Intelligence Product (Henley-Putnam).pdf#page=2|BLUF handout, p. 2]]). Kaiser and Pherson note that analytic writing borrows this convention from journalism, unlike academic writing, which builds toward its conclusion ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 4 (scan).pdf#page=5|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 23]]).

## Key Points
**The BLUF handout**
- **The umbrella:** a good BLUF covers all the information in its paragraph "like an umbrella"; anything outside it moves to another paragraph, or the BLUF changes ([[BLUF Writing Format - How to Write an Intelligence Product (Henley-Putnam).pdf#page=2|BLUF handout, p. 2]]).
- **Parts of a product:** title, executive summary (in the example, [[Key Judgments]]), introduction, background, analysis, context, and outlook. The title, executive summary, and introduction are written last, because they summarize everything else ([[BLUF Writing Format - How to Write an Intelligence Product (Henley-Putnam).pdf#page=1|BLUF handout, pp. 1–7]]).
- **The outlook** sums up the paper and should never introduce new ideas ([[BLUF Writing Format - How to Write an Intelligence Product (Henley-Putnam).pdf#page=6|BLUF handout, p. 6]]).
- **The title** is "the ultimate BLUF" ([[BLUF Writing Format - How to Write an Intelligence Product (Henley-Putnam).pdf#page=7|BLUF handout, p. 7]]). See [[Analytic Title]].
- **No editorializing:** the analysis must rest on facts analyzed logically and, as far as possible, without bias ([[BLUF Writing Format - How to Write an Intelligence Product (Henley-Putnam).pdf#page=3|BLUF handout, p. 3]]).
- **Accuracy, brevity, and clarity** leave nothing to interpretation and reduce the chance a decision maker misreads the analysis ([[BLUF Writing Format - How to Write an Intelligence Product (Henley-Putnam).pdf#page=4|BLUF handout, p. 4]]).

**Kaiser and Pherson**
- A message-driven paper puts the title, lead sentence, and key judgments first, and the most important part of most assessments comes at the end, in the outlook and implications; see [[Inverted Triangle]] ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 4 (scan).pdf#page=5|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 23]]).

**The Intro to Intelligence slides**
- The first sentence of an article states its recommendation or implications, and the first sentences of its paragraphs form an outline of its key points ([[SRC - Intelligence Writing (Williams and Molzon Slides)|Williams & Molzon, slides 4, 6]]).
- ICD 203 likewise requires a "clear main analytic message up front" ([[ODNI - ICD 203, Analytic Standards (2015).pdf#page=4|ICD 203, §D.6.e(6)]]).

> [!caution] Accuracy check
> The Williams and Molzon slide cites ICD 203 section D.6.e(5) for this rule ([[SRC - Intelligence Writing (Williams and Molzon Slides)|Williams & Molzon, slide 4]]). In the directive, (5) is customer relevance; the main analytic message up front is in (6) ([[ODNI - ICD 203, Analytic Standards (2015).pdf#page=4|ICD 203, §D.6.e(5)–(6)]]).
