> [!summary]
> Joseph Gartin, a former dean of CIA's Sherman Kent School, argues that [[Intelligence Analysis|intelligence analysis]] "is at an inflection point".[^1] Much of it still comes down to "read stuff, write stuff," much as in [[Sherman Kent]]'s day, while AI, big data, and [[Machine Learning|machine learning]] reshape the field.[^2] ==He urges the IC to make data-driven analysis mainstream and to measure and reward accuracy==.[^3]

## Key Points
- **Kent's legacy:** Kent envisioned "an elevated debate" among the finest minds.[^1] Repeated failures, from the 1962 Cuba estimate to the fall of the Shah and Iraq WMD, show the weakness of relying on "very smart (and very similar) people" to read reports and make accurate assessments.[^4]
- **Five drivers of change**:[^5]
	1. [[Structured Analytic Technique|Structured analytic techniques]], with [[Richards Heuer]]'s *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis* (1999) the benchmark.
	2. Cognitive science and behavioral economics.
	3. Quantitative forecasting, such as Philip Tetlock's work and [[Prediction Market|prediction markets]].
	4. Counterterrorism's demand, since [[September 11 Attacks (2001)|9/11]], for hyper-specific analysis of individuals and networks.
	5. The explosion of data.
- **A 2030 scenario**:[^6] an analyst's digital assistant brings her a prediction market's odds, a base rate, and news that a source she used as a [[Linchpin Evidence|linchpin]] has been exposed as a fabricator. It also reports that her personal accuracy rating has fallen to 47 percent.
- **Five suggestions**:[^3]
	1. Embrace data-driven analysis as mainstream analysis.
	2. Make sure structured techniques deliver measurable improvements: "Blind faith in SATs is no more redemptive than any other blind faith."
	3. Shorten the feedback loop.
	4. Measure and reward accuracy.
	5. Hire, develop, and keep agile talent.

> [!question] Whose view is this?
> These are Gartin's personal views; the journal notes they are not official.[^1]
