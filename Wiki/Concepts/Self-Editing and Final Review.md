> [!summary]
> Kaiser and Pherson count a finished first draft as only about halfway to submission. Review takes at least two stages, starting from the first word, and ==the key is to read the draft as the reader, not the author==.[^1] Even minor mistakes lead readers to question the analysis.[^1]

## Key Points
- **Use a printed copy;** some writers read the draft aloud.[^1]
- **The final review, in order**:[^2]
	1. The title: does it capture the "what?" and "so what?", and can it be shorter? See [[Analytic Title]].
	2. The [[Topic Sentence|topic sentences]] alone: does the storyline advance with each one?
	3. The order within paragraphs, and whether any paragraph is a "word brick" or a grocery list of bullets.
	4. Terms and background the reader may need.
	5. Spelling and grammar, on a printed copy.
- **Five common grammatical errors:** misused relative pronouns, subject-verb disagreement, pronouns that disagree with their antecedents, possessive pronouns confused with contractions (avoid contractions in analytic writing), and "however" used to join two independent clauses.[^3]
- **Spell check is not enough:** it missed "carnal" written for "causal" in the authors' own work.[^4]
- **Take a break** before self-editing; distance helps you catch errors.[^4]
- **Get a colleague's review:** the more important and widely distributed the paper, the more critical it is.[^5]
- **Everyone needs an editor,** because "no one writes a good first draft".[^6]

## Why It Matters
- The class notes give the same advice: be a ruthless editor, and leave a draft for at least 30 minutes before rereading it. See [[Intelligence Writing Style]] and [[Economizing on Words]].
