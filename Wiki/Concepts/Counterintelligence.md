> [!summary]
> Lowenthal, as summarized in Intro to Intelligence's grammar summaries, uses Executive Order 12333's definition of counterintelligence: "information gathered and activities conducted to identify, deceive, exploit, disrupt or protect" against espionage and other activities by foreign states or non-state adversaries. He divides it into three types: collection, defensive, and offensive.[^1] The course's handout on IC organizations names the FBI as the head of U.S. counterintelligence.[^2]

## Key Points
**Three types**[^1]
- **Collection:** gaining information about an opponent's intelligence collection capabilities.
- **Defensive:** thwarting efforts by hostile intelligence services to penetrate one's own service.
- **Offensive:** once an opponent's efforts are identified, ==manipulating them by turning the opponent's agents into double agents or feeding them false information==.

**Tools and problems**[^3]
- **[[Damage Assessment (Counterintelligence)|Damage assessment]]:** once a spy is identified, working out what intelligence was compromised.[^4]
- **[[Insider Threat|Insider threats]]:** many of the greatest U.S. spies were homegrown; a 2011 executive order created the Insider Threat Program.[^5]
- **[[National Security Letter|National security letters]]:** administrative subpoenas for records, with a gag order.[^6]
- **[[Originator Controlled|ORCON]]:** a marking that requires the originating agency's approval to pass intelligence on; it protects sources and methods but may slow sharing.[^7]

## Why It Matters
- The 9/11 Commission describes how fear of Soviet penetration, from James Jesus Angleton's search for moles to the Aldrich Ames case, led the CIA to compartment information, which "vastly complicated information sharing".[^8]
- Intro to Intelligence's polygraph lecture listed the counterintelligence topics an examination covers: espionage or sabotage against the U.S., intentionally compromising classified information, involvement in terrorist activity, secret foreign contacts, and deliberate damage to U.S. government IT systems.[^9] See [[Polygraph Examination]].
- Counterintelligence is one of the seven mission objectives of the [[National Intelligence Strategy]].[^10]
