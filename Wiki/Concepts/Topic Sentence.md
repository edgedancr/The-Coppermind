> [!summary]
> A topic sentence opens a paragraph and tells the reader what it covers. Intro to Intelligence's SI Writing Lab taught that ==99% of people will not read a whole report, only its topic sentences and summary==, which is why topic sentences matter so much ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/3/26|Intro notes, 9/3/26]]). A strong one makes causation explicit and gives the paragraph a roadmap ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/3/26|Intro notes, 9/3/26]], [[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/15/26|Intro notes, 9/15/26]]).

## Key Points
**Three types** ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/3/26|Intro notes, 9/3/26]])
- **General:** names the main idea of the paragraph but stays broad.
- **Clueing:** names the main idea and signals that several details will follow, without naming them ("I gave my parents three reasons for wanting a raise in my allowance").
- **Specific (the analytical gold standard):** names the main idea and lists the sub-topics or analytical categories to be covered, giving a tight roadmap and guaranteeing paragraph unity.

**Common faults** ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/3/26|Intro notes, 9/3/26]], [[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/15/26|Intro notes, 9/15/26]])
- **Merely a fact:** a static or historical fact gives no analytical path forward.
- **Overly broad or vague.**
- **Too specific:** stats, source attributions, and narrow examples belong in the body as evidence, not at the head.

**What makes one strong:** not length, but making causation explicit ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/3/26|Intro notes, 9/3/26]]). Most strong sentences combine ideas with four patterns ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/3/26|Intro notes, 9/3/26]]):
- **Coordination** joins ideas of roughly equal weight (*and, but, or, yet*).
- **Subordination** ranks one idea under another or sequences them (*because, although, when, if, since, after*). "There is no such thing as an unordered list": readers always rank items, so the writer must rank them on purpose. *Although* works as an inverter.
- **Apposition** renames a noun for efficiency: "The committee chair, **a former auditor,** demanded revisions."
- **Modification** compresses information into adjective, adverb, participial, or prepositional phrases.

**Example** ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/15/26|Intro notes, 9/15/26]])
- *Weak:* "Many things are happening in the world today that affect our country's national security."
- *Strong:* "Contemporary US national security planning is primarily challenged by Russian cyber warfare, Chinese industrial espionage, and Iranian regional proxy networks."

## Why It Matters
- Lead the reader by the hand: a reader who wanted to do their own analysis would do it anyway ([[Intro to Intelligence Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/8/26|Intro notes, 9/8/26]]).
- SAIL's version of the same idea: put together, the topic sentences of every paragraph should form a mini essay ([[SAIL Class Notes (Fall 2026)#9/8/26|SAIL notes, 9/8/26]]). See [[Essay Types]] and [[Intelligence Writing Style]].
