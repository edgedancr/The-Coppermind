> [!summary]
> Finding no taxonomy of the variables that affect intelligence analysis, Rob Johnston built one, first published in *Studies in Intelligence* in 2003 ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=18|Johnston, pp. xviii, 33]]). It sorts the variables into four groups: systemic, systematic, idiosyncratic, and communicative ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=58|Johnston, pp. 39–42]]). ==The taxonomy "is purely descriptive"==: it does not weigh the variables or predict their effects, but offers a framework for gathering existing research and planning experiments ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=58|Johnston, p. 39]]).

## Key Points
**How he built it** ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=57|Johnston, pp. 38–39]])
- Reviewed 2,432 texts, narrowed them to 374, and sorted the variables each author named into groups (a "Q-sort").
- Refined the list through interviews with 51 experts and 39 novices and two focus groups.
- Tested it by observing analysts in a controlled training environment, including exercises he joined himself.

**The four groups**
1. **Systemic variables:** the organization and its environment: structure, leadership, culture (history, traditions, social practice, taboo), resources and incentives, and external factors such as consumer needs, time imperatives, politics, security, and adversaries' denial and deception ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=58|Johnston, pp. 39–40]]).
2. **Systematic variables:** the analytic process itself: user requirements, how information was acquired, its reliability and validity, how it is stored and retrieved, analytic methods (intuitive, structured, or semi-structured), decision strategies, and reporting ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=59|Johnston, p. 40]]).
3. **Idiosyncratic variables:** the individual analyst: *Weltanschauung* (world view) from familial, cultural, ethnic, religious, linguistic, and political affiliations; psychology, including biases, personality, [[Cognitive Load|cognitive load]], expertise, and reaction to stress; and education, training, and readiness ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=60|Johnston, p. 41]]).
4. **Communicative variables:** formal and informal communication within and among groups and organizations, from products to e-mails, and the technology for networked collaboration ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=61|Johnston, p. 42]]).

**Its definition of analysis:** reviewing definitions from Kent to Heuer, Johnston concludes that analysis is a socio-cognitive process in a secret domain; see [[Intelligence Analysis]] ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=54|Johnston, pp. 35–37]]).

## How to Use It
- As a research map: Johnston suggests it could seed a research agenda, training programs, a lessons-learned database, and tools for collaborative forecasting ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=63|Johnston, p. 44]]).
- He notes that the real variables are more fluid and interconnected than a hierarchical list suggests ([[Johnston - Analytic Culture in the US Intelligence Community (2005).pdf#page=56|Johnston, p. 37]]); for one dynamic view, see [[Systems Model of the Intelligence Cycle]].
- Compare [[Analytic Culture]], whose findings fall mostly under the systemic and systematic variables.
