> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission argued that because "imagination is not a gift usually associated with bureaucracies," government must find a way of "routinizing, even bureaucratizing, the exercise of imagination" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=361|9/11 Report, p. 344]]). It found that the warning methods the Intelligence Community had developed since Pearl Harbor ==did not fail against al-Qaeda: "they were not really tried"== ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=364|9/11 Report, pp. 347–348]]).

## Components
**The four common elements** of methods for avoiding surprise attack ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=363|9/11 Report, p. 346]])
1. Think about how surprise attacks might be launched.
2. Identify telltale indicators connected to the most dangerous possibilities.
3. Where feasible, collect intelligence on those indicators.
4. Adopt defenses to deflect the most dangerous possibilities, or at least trigger earlier warning.

**What was not done about aircraft as weapons** (the CIA's [[Counterterrorist Center (CIA)|Counterterrorist Center]], ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=364|9/11 Report, p. 347]]))
1. It did not analyze how a hijacked or explosives-laden aircraft might be used as a weapon, or do [[Red Team Analysis|red team analysis]]; the Commission believes this would have spotlighted the terrorists' constraint of finding a suicide operative able to fly large jets.
2. It set no indicators, such as possible terrorists seeking training on large jets.
3. No collection requirements were set, so a July 2001 FBI report on aviation training in Arizona and Moussaoui's August 2001 arrest had "no effect on warning."
4. No one analyzed systemic defenses within or against aircraft; the FAA reacted only to specific, credible threats.

## How to Use It
- The scenario was "imaginable, and imagined" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=362|9/11 Report, p. 345]]): a 1999 FAA intelligence paper named a "suicide hijacking operation" but judged it unlikely ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=362|9/11 Report, p. 345]]), and NORAD set aside an exercise of a hijacked airliner hitting the Pentagon as "too unrealistic" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=363|9/11 Report, p. 346]]).
- The Commission's challenge: "flesh out and test those scenarios, then figure out a way to turn a scenario into constructive action" ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=363|9/11 Report, p. 346]]).

## Limits and Cautions
- Measures from a 1992 warning study for DCI Robert Gates "languished," and responsibility for terrorism warning passed from the national intelligence officer for warning to the Counterterrorist Center ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=363|9/11 Report, p. 346]]).
- Richard Clarke told the Commission he owed his awareness of the aircraft danger more to Tom Clancy novels than to intelligence warnings ([[National Commission on Terrorist Attacks - The 9-11 Commission Report (2004).pdf#page=364|9/11 Report, p. 347]]).
- Compare the Pearl Harbor case: [[Pearl Harbor (1941)]].
