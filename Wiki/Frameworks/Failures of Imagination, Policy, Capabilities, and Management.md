> [!summary]
> The 9/11 Commission concluded that ==the 9/11 attacks "revealed four kinds of failures: in imagination, policy, capabilities, and management"==.[^1] It reached that judgment with a caution about hindsight, quoting Roberta Wohlstetter on [[Pearl Harbor (1941)|Pearl Harbor]]: after the event a signal "is always crystal clear," but before it, it is "obscure and pregnant with conflicting meanings".[^1]

## Components
- **Imagination**[^2]
	- The Commission judged that Presidents Clinton and Bush understood [[Usama Bin Ladin|bin Ladin]] was a danger, but not "how many people [[Al-Qaeda|al Qaeda]] might kill, and how soon"; the threat "had not yet become compelling".[^3]
	- The July 1995 [[National Intelligence Estimate]] warned of attacks in the United States; its 1997 update mentioned bin Ladin in three sentences and was the last estimate on terrorism before 9/11.[^4] See [[Institutionalizing Imagination]].
- **Policy**[^5]
	- Al-Qaeda fit neither existing category: trial and punishment for individuals, or sanction, reprisal, deterrence, or war for hostile governments.[^6]
	- Policymakers turned mainly to the CIA and [[Covert Action|covert action]]; the Defense Department was not given the mission of ending al-Qaeda's [[Terrorist Sanctuary|sanctuary]] in Afghanistan.[^7]
	- "It is hardest to mount a major effort while a problem still seems minor".[^8]
- **Capabilities**[^9]
	- The government met al-Qaeda with the institutions and capabilities of the late Cold War, which were insufficient.[^10]
	- The most serious weaknesses were domestic: the FBI could not link its field agents' knowledge to national priorities.[^11]
- **Management**[^12]
	- Information was not shared, analysis was not pooled, and handoffs were lost across the foreign-domestic divide.[^13]
	- The Commission's image: specialists in a hospital, each ordering tests, with no "attending physician" making them work as a team.[^13]
	- The DCI "has to direct agencies without controlling them".[^14] See [[Director of National Intelligence]].

**Operational opportunities:** the Commission lists ten missed chances from January 2000 to August 2001, mostly CIA and FBI failures to watchlist or share what they knew about two future hijackers, the Kuala Lumpur meeting, Zacarias Moussaoui, and [[Khalid Sheikh Mohammed]].[^15]

## How to Use It
- **The millennium exception:** in December 1999 information "flowed widely and abundantly" and the government acted in concert; the Commission credits shared information at least as much as worry over Y2K.[^16] Afterward, counterterrorism "went back to being a secret preserve".[^17] See [[Need to Share]].

## Limits and Cautions
- The Commission asked whether its insights "would really have been meaningful at the time",[^1] and doubted that watchlisting alone would have prevented the attacks.[^18]
- For the attacks themselves, see [[September 11 Attacks (2001)]].
