> [!summary]
> Intro to Intelligence teaches 13 themes in U.S. intelligence, ==persistent issues from history that keep coming back==, grouped as organization, people, process, and technology.[^1] The course defines each one mostly in the words of Mark Lowenthal ("L") and Gregory Treverton ("T"), with textbook page numbers.[^2] 

## Components
Taglines are from [[SRC - SI Themes and Pearl Harbor Context|SI Themes]]; definitions from the theme summary, cited by theme number.

**Organization**
1. **Role of culture** ("Importance of IC & national security organizations and understanding foreign cultures"). The CIA's operations culture is powerful but "fundamentally at odds with accountability";[^3] its standard is "good enough to act," the FBI's "beyond a reasonable doubt".[^4] Foreign cultures complicate U.S. decisions too (the instructor's note).[^5] See [[Seven Tribes of Intelligence]].
2. **Collection synergy** ("There are few 'silver bullets' in intelligence"). One discipline tips off another, and combining them offsets each one's weaknesses.[^6] See [[All-Source Intelligence]].
3. **Redundant analytic structure** ("Importance of [[Competitive Analysis|competitive analysis]]"). See [[Redundant Analytic Structure]].[^7]

**People**

4. **Strategic vs. tactical intelligence** ("The long view and the short-term view"). Policymakers need tailored, timely background, warning, and assessments; tactical users need "dominant battlefield awareness" in near real time.[^8] See [[Levels of Intelligence]].
5. **Policymakers' use of intelligence** ("How intelligence gets used—and abused"). See [[Consumer-Producer Relations]].[^9]
6. **Politicization** ("[[Speaking Truth to Power|Speaking truth to power]] vs. giving them what they want"). See [[Politicized Intelligence]].[^10]

**Process**

7. **Consumer-producer relations** ("Understanding user information needs"). See [[Consumer-Producer Relations]].[^11]
8. **Secrecy vs. openness** ("Information sharing and protection of sources and methods"). Secrecy is one reason intelligence agencies exist, but it clashes with the openness of representative democracy.[^12]
9. **[[Denial and Deception]]** ("Preventing and misdirecting intelligence collection and analysis").[^13]
10. **[[Mirror Imaging]]** ("Assuming 'they' think or do things just like we do").[^14]

**Technology**

11. **Reliance on technology for collection** ("The American way of war and intelligence is with machines"). The U.S. has relied on technical collection since the 1940s, partly because the Soviet Union was closed;[^15] now most of the world can be seen directly, "eyeball INT".[^16]
12. **Reliance on technology for analysis and dissemination** ("The IT system is 'the solution'"). Treverton: IT is a means, not an end, and "cannot make sharing happen on its own".[^17]
13. **Encryption** ("Technical solutions to information security and privacy issues"). Cheap, strong encryption will increasingly limit SIGINT to [[Traffic Analysis|traffic analysis]].[^18]

## How to Use It
- In a case study, ask which themes it shows. The course's theme handout is titled "SI Themes & Pearl Harbor Context," pairing the themes with that case.[^19] See [[Pearl Harbor (1941)]].

## Limits and Cautions
- The definitions quote two textbooks (Lowenthal's 5th edition and Treverton's 2003 book) that aren't in the vault, so their page numbers can't be checked here.[^20]
