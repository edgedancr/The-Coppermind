> [!summary]
> RAND researchers Jim Mitre and Joel B. Predd propose five hard problems that the possible emergence of [[Artificial General Intelligence|artificial general intelligence]] (AGI) poses for U.S. national security.[^1] They offer them as ==a common language for debating AGI's risks and opportunities, and a rubric for evaluating strategies==, because proposals that advance one problem can undermine or ignore another.[^1] Reach for it when judging whether an AI strategy is built for only one possible future.

## Components
**Overarching: endemic uncertainty.** It is unclear whether performance will keep scaling with compute (see [[Scaling Laws]]), where the threshold for AGI lies, or whether it will arrive suddenly or gradually; developers might not know a critical threshold has been crossed until it has.[^2]

1. **Wonder weapons.** AGI might produce a breakthrough with a decisive first-mover advantage, such as a "splendid first cyber strike," highly accurate prediction for military operations, or dominant autonomous weapons, or a "fog-of-war machine" that makes battlefield information untrustworthy.[^3]
2. **Systemic shifts in power.** AGI might upend military balances (hiders versus finders, precision versus mass), threaten democratic decision-making through advanced propaganda, or displace labor so fast that GDP soars while wages collapse.[^3]
3. **Nonexperts empowered to build weapons of mass destruction.** Models can act as "malicious mentors" that turn complex methods, for example for a pathogen or cyber malware, into accessible instructions, though knowing how is not the same as building.[^3]
4. **Artificial entities with agency.** Growing reliance on AI may erode human agency; AGI could act on the world stage, be misaligned with its designers' intentions, or, in a loss-of-control scenario, resist being turned off.[^3] See [[AI Alignment]].
5. **Instability.** The race for AGI could raise tensions like the nuclear arms race, in which perceived capabilities and rivals' intentions matter as much as actual capabilities.[^3]

## How to Use It
1. Test a proposed AI strategy against all five: does progress on one problem come at the cost of another?[^3]
2. Don't bet on a single future; the authors call any strategy over-optimized for one paradigm "a high-risk proposition".[^2]
3. The authors' recommendations:[^4] keep "no-regret" options that make sense in any future (STEM education, situational awareness, protecting model weights, and safety research); develop high-regret options and contingency plans before they're needed; and run scenario exercises on post-AGI shifts in military power and the economy.

## Limits and Cautions
- The authors say the problems overlap and may not cover everything policymakers will face.[^3]
- The problems rest on different assumptions. The wonder-weapon problem assumes the most ambitious ones, which posit high-consequence events of unknown probability.[^3]
- The paper defines AGI as human-level or even superhuman intelligence across a wide variety of cognitive tasks,[^5] which differs from SAIL's definition; see [[Artificial General Intelligence]].
