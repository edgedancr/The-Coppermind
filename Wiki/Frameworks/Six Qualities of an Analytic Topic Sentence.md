> [!summary]
> Kaiser and Pherson's first rule for a topic sentence in an analytic paper is that it "includes analysis": a judgment that helps the reader follow the argument.[^1] Because it is a judgment, it carries some uncertainty, shown with [[Estimative Language|caveats]] like "probably," verbs like "suggest," or conditions beginning with "if".[^1] ==A well-written analytic topic sentence is also precise, clear, accurate, consistent, complete, and digestible==.[^2]

## Key Points
**Without an analytic topic sentence**[^3]
- Readers must draw their own conclusions, and may reach different ones; the writer becomes a researcher collating information.
- The paragraph drifts, and the paper loses the thread that connects its paragraphs.

**Six types**:[^4] summarization, generalization, comparison, causation, evaluation, and future assessment.

**Six qualities**[^2]
1. **Precise:** open to only one interpretation. Precision is not the same as clarity: a sentence can be clear but only hint at its point.[^5]
2. **Clear:** free of convoluted language, internal inconsistencies, reliance on context, passive voice, and emphasis on background.[^6] See [[Active Voice]].
3. **Accurate:** the judgment captures the supporting information correctly.
4. **Consistent:** treats the issue the same way the supporting information does.
5. **Complete:** covers all the main analytic issues in the paragraph.
6. **Digestible:** readable without notes or a flow chart; a sentence with too many ideas means the paragraph should be split.[^7]

**Building the paragraph**
- The topic sentence and its supporting information should mesh "like the cogs" of two machined gears.[^2]
- Put the main point first and the context after it, and avoid opening with a long dependent clause.[^8]

## Why It Matters
- It adds an analytic standard to the class's three types of [[Topic Sentence]]; the [[Six-Step Paragraph Review]] checks it.
