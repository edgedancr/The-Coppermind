> [!summary]
> Kaiser and Pherson's first rule for a topic sentence in an analytic paper is that it "includes analysis": a judgment that helps the reader follow the argument ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=3|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 29]]). Because it is a judgment, it carries some uncertainty, shown with [[Estimative Language|caveats]] like "probably," verbs like "suggest," or conditions beginning with "if" ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=3|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 29]]). ==A well-written analytic topic sentence is also precise, clear, accurate, consistent, complete, and digestible== ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=5|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 31]]).

## Key Points
**Without an analytic topic sentence** ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=3|Kaiser & Pherson, pp. 29–30]])
- Readers must draw their own conclusions, and may reach different ones; the writer becomes a researcher collating information.
- The paragraph drifts, and the paper loses the thread that connects its paragraphs.

**Six types** ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=4|Kaiser & Pherson, pp. 30–31]]): summarization, generalization, comparison, causation, evaluation, and future assessment.

**Six qualities** ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=5|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 31]])
1. **Precise:** open to only one interpretation. Precision is not the same as clarity: a sentence can be clear but only hint at its point ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=5|Kaiser & Pherson, pp. 31–32]]).
2. **Clear:** free of convoluted language, internal inconsistencies, reliance on context, passive voice, and emphasis on background ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=7|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 33]]). See [[Active Voice]].
3. **Accurate:** the judgment captures the supporting information correctly.
4. **Consistent:** treats the issue the same way the supporting information does.
5. **Complete:** covers all the main analytic issues in the paragraph.
6. **Digestible:** readable without notes or a flow chart; a sentence with too many ideas means the paragraph should be split ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=12|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 38]]).

**Building the paragraph**
- The topic sentence and its supporting information should mesh "like the cogs" of two machined gears ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=5|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 31]]).
- Put the main point first and the context after it, and avoid opening with a long dependent clause ([[Kaiser and Pherson - Analytic Writing Guide, ch. 5-7 (scan).pdf#page=9|Kaiser & Pherson, p. 35]]).
