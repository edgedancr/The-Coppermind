> [!summary]
> Before outlining, Kaiser and Pherson set a paper's analytic direction with five focal questions: "what?", "why now?", impact so far, outlook, and implications. ==Writers should keep that order in most cases==, because it builds the knowledge readers need to take in the later sections.[^1] A paper need not answer all five.[^2]

## Key Points
- **What has changed?** An event, a condition, a decision, a view or perception, or a behavioral norm. The change from yesterday is usually the first focus of an analytic paper.[^1]
- **Why now?** The factors behind the "what." The key word is "because".[^1] Drivers include interests and objectives; economic and technological developments; cultural, social, and religious forces; historical experience; personality traits; group dynamics; and beliefs and perceptions.[^3]
- **Impact so far:** a separate section mainly when the impact is an intermediate result that sets up something different, or to separate one actor's moves from another's response.[^4]
- **Outlook:** the analyst explains possibilities rather than predicting, names the main drivers, and says which [[Indicators and Signposts|indicators]] to watch. The key word is "if." Outlooks may be single or multiple, with or without a time frame.[^5]
- **Implications:** how the issue affects the target audience and what opportunities and options exist, written from the perspective of how the main actor would respond, not as advice on what the audience should do. Consequences for neighbors or competitors belong in the outlook.[^2]

**Choosing which questions to answer**[^6]
- What development drives the paper? Where to begin is the most critical decision.
- What are the target audience's interests and needs, and how much does it already know?
- What questions will the audience ask?
- What information do you have?

## Why It Matters
- It shapes the [[Detailed Outline]] and the message-driven order of the [[Inverted Triangle]]. Compare the five policymaker questions in [[Limits of Prediction]].
